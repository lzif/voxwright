## Candidates

### 1. Compound site-plan marking — the whole estate drawn on the ground first (NEW — extends T2) — HIGH
- **Principle:** T2 says "mark the footprint before placing anything" — for one
  house. This build scales that to the whole compound: before any vertical
  construction, every zone (perimeter, buildings, courtyard) is drawn on the grass
  as a flat outline plan, so the composition is decided in plan view, not
  discovered wall by wall.
- **Recipe:** On flat grass, lay the site plan as 1-wide outline lines in
  contrasting materials (tan/orange guide lines first, inked to dark brown for the
  perimeter; white lines for building/courtyard zones). Each rectangle is one
  future zone: main hall, wings, gatehouse, courtyard, garden. `[v2: annotate key
  dimensions on the plan — this build labels 33 / 53 / 40 / 47 over the plan
  (f0004–f0008).]` Build zones in sequence against the plan — the lines stay
  visible until their zone is built, then get absorbed into floors/paths
  (confirmed: plan lines persist for unbuilt zones at f0024, f0053–f0055). Keep
  the plan legible: outlines only, no fill — a filled zone reads as a floor, not
  a plan.
- **Apply when:** compounds, estates, villages, any multi-building site.
  **NOT when:** a single standalone building (T2's corner-marking is enough); on
  sloped terrain (the plan lines break across terraces — flatten the site first
  or plan per terrace).
- **Prevents:** the compound that grows by accident — wings at wrong angles, a
  courtyard too small for its buildings, a gate that lands on nothing.
- (Dense ref: f0001–f0008 — full zone outlines + dimension numbers before any
  walls exist; f0024/f0053–f0055 — plan lines still visible mid-construction;
  finished wall and gatehouse sit exactly on the planned lines, f0066–f0068.)
- `[v2: v1's inference flag (c) — "zone-to-rectangle mapping approximate" — is
  RESOLVED. The dense top-down sequence (f0001–f0008) plus late plan lines
  (f0024, f0053–f0055) plus the finished showcase (f0066–f0068) let each rectangle
  be matched 1:1 to its built zone: top = gatehouse, center = main hall, left +
  right = red side halls, bottom = front gatehouse.]`

### 2. East-Asian tiled roof grammar — layered eave bands + upturned corners (NEW) — HIGH
- **Principle:** T4 covers dark stair-block roofs and two-tone striping, but no
  East-Asian roof construction. This roof's read comes from three things working
  together: the dark tile field, a stacked eave band, and corners that sweep
  upward instead of stopping flat.
- **Recipe:** Roof field of dark stair blocks (deepslate tile family), hip or gable
  profile, overhanging the walls by 1–2 blocks. At the eave, stack 2–3 visible
  bands: dark tile overhang on top, a saturated red trim band beneath it, and a
  timber fascia band beneath that — the bands are what make the eave read as
  layered joinery rather than a slab edge. White rafter-tail blocks under the
  eave (f0010–f0011) sharpen the stack. At each corner, step the eave courses
  upward 1 block over the last 2–3 blocks of run so the corner lifts — subtle
  (1 block of rise), never a horn. Finish the ridge with short red accent blocks
  at intervals (red ridge ticks) or a continuous red ridge band on accent
  buildings. `[v2: on two-story halls, stack TWO roof tiers — a lower eave roof
  over the ground-floor veranda plus an upper roof over the second story
  (f0046/f0048).]`
- **Apply when:** Japanese/Chinese/Korean-inspired builds, temple compounds, any
  build where the roof is the primary silhouette. **NOT when:** European medieval
  (use T4's gables + T15's arches); on small roofs <5 wide (the band stack eats
  the whole roof — use a single trim band instead).
- **Prevents:** the "dark lid" roof — a flat dark slab that caps the building
  without detailing; and the straight-triangle gable on an East-Asian build,
  which reads as the wrong continent.
- (Dense ref: f0010–f0012 — eave stack on the gatehouse; f0015/f0016/f0063 — eave
  stack on the wall wing; f0046/f0048 — two-tier roof + shoji upper story on the
  main hall; f0049–f0052 — red ridge ticks/band + stepped gable on the red hall;
  f0019 — subtle 1-block corner lift on the gatehouse.)

### 3. Roofed compound wall + gatehouse pavilion (NEW) — HIGH
- **Principle:** The compound's edge is not a fence or a bare wall — it's
  architecture: a roofed corridor wall with a gatehouse pavilion breaking it. The
  wall carries the compound's palette (white plaster, red trim, dark tile roof)
  so the boundary reads as part of the build, not as a property line.
- **Recipe:** Perimeter wall: white plaster face, 1 red trim band at the base and
  1 under the eave, dark tiled roof capping the full run, sitting on a white
  stone footing course. Height ~3–4 blocks plus roof. At the entrance, break the
  wall with a gatehouse pavilion: 4 timber pillars on a white stone platform,
  open on all sides, carrying its own tiled roof with the layered eave
  (candidate 2) — the pavilion is the door, not a hole in the wall. Keep the
  wall's roofline continuous except at the gatehouse, where the pavilion roof
  steps up above it.
- **Apply when:** walled compounds, temple grounds, estates, palace sites.
  **NOT when:** villages (open edges read as lived-in — a roofed wall around a
  village reads as a fortress); single houses (the wall dwarfs the house — use a
  garden fence or hedge instead, T5).
- **Prevents:** the fence-line compound — a bare wall that makes the estate read
  as a prison yard; and the hole-in-the-wall gate, which reads as damage rather
  than arrival.
- (Dense ref: f0013–f0020 — wall wing extending from the gatehouse; f0021–f0023
  — wall corner + gatehouse complete; f0063 — wall wing close-up, all bands;
  f0064–f0065 — gatehouse from inside: open timber pillars, white platform +
  steps; f0066–f0068 — full perimeter in the finished showcase.)

### 4. Tile-and-sand courtyard grid — formal sand rectangles inset in a tile field (NEW) — HIGH
- **Principle:** T8's zen bed is restraint (one sand bed, 3–5 elements, negative
  space). This courtyard is the formal counterpart: a white tile field with
  MULTIPLE sand rectangles inset in dark borders, composed as a grid — the sand
  is a paving material here, not a meditation object.
- **Recipe:** Pave the courtyard in white tiles (quartz/white concrete family).
  Inset 2–4 sand rectangles (yellow sand/gravel), each bordered by a 1-wide dark
  stone course, aligned to the courtyard's geometry (parallel to the main hall,
  never diagonal). Place exactly ONE vertical feature in the courtyard — here a
  central dark pillar — and keep all other verticals (lanterns, pots) at the
  courtyard's edges. Edge the courtyard with a low stone curb + bamboo rail
  caps, and enter it by a wide ceremonial stair (5–6 wide, stone, bamboo-railed
  curbs). `[v2: curb railing recipe — yellow bamboo rails on white stone posts,
  red lanterns on curb posts at intervals (f0027/f0031).]`
- **Apply when:** formal courtyards, temple forecourts, palace grounds.
  **NOT when:** the garden story is wild/restrained (use T8's zen bed — one bed,
  negative space); small courtyards <7 wide (the grid chops the space into
  postage stamps — use a single sand bed instead).
- **Prevents:** the empty-tile plaza (a big white floor with nothing in it reads
  as unfinished) and the sand-pit courtyard (unbordered sand reads as a beach,
  not a garden).
- (Dense ref: f0027 — white tile field, two dark-bordered sand rectangles,
  bamboo-railed curb, red curb lanterns, wide stair; f0031/f0033 — same courtyard
  from other angles, sand grid + curb + stair confirmed.)

### 5. Vermillion pavilion PAIR — one saturated color as the compound anchor, on twin flanking halls (NEW — color-anchor rule, T10 family) — HIGH — [v2 CORRECTED]
- **Principle:** T10 says one oversized anchor justifies the theme. This is the
  color version: in a monochrome compound (white + dark + timber), paint the
  flanking side halls in a saturated accent — vermillion red on columns, beams,
  and trim — and they become the compound's focal pair without any size
  advantage.
- **Recipe:** Pick the secondary halls flanking the main hall (never the main
  hall — the main hall owns the size hierarchy). Paint their full structural
  systems in the accent: columns, beams, eave trim, door/window frames —
  structure, not decoration. Keep their walls white and their roofs dark like
  the rest of the compound, so the accent reads as "the same buildings, in red"
  rather than a different style. One accent color only, applied symmetrically —
  a single red hall reads as an accident; the pair reads as a composition.
  `[v2 CORRECTION: v1 said "exactly ONE pavilion" and "a second red building
  splits the focal read instantly." The dense set refutes that: this compound has
  TWO red-accented side halls (f0053–f0055, f0066–f0068), symmetric around the
  main hall, and the composition holds. The rule is one accent COLOR, on the
  symmetric flanking pair — not one accent building.]`
- **Apply when:** compounds with 2+ halls, temple complexes, palace sites.
  **NOT when:** single buildings (a lone red house is just a red house — the
  anchor needs a monochrome context to contrast against); palettes that already
  run 3+ saturated colors (the accent drowns).
- **Prevents:** the flat compound — three white halls in a row read as a
  barracks; red flanking halls give the eye destinations on both sides of the
  main hall.
- (Dense ref: f0049–f0052 — fully red-columned hall on its stepped white plinth;
  f0053–f0055 — BOTH red halls visible flanking the main hall; f0066–f0068 —
  twin red halls confirmed in the finished showcase.)

### 6. Shoji panel windows — wood frame + pale infill, no glass (T7 VARIANT — folds into T7, not standalone) — HIGH
- **Principle:** T7's window recipe is panes + shutters + flower box, with a
  mullion variant (dark cross over glass). This is the East-Asian window
  language: a timber frame grid with a pale opaque infill panel — no glass, no
  shutters, no flower box. The window reads as a lit paper screen, not a glazed
  opening.
- **Recipe:** Frame the opening in timber (posts + lintel, same wood as the
  building's frame). Fill it with a pale panel (white concrete/quartz or light
  wood planks) — flat, no glass panes. Repeat the panel in a vertical column per
  bay, aligned to the pillar grid. At night the panels read as lit screens if a
  warm light sits one block behind them (inferred — no night frame in this reel;
  all 68 dense frames are day).
- **Apply when:** Japanese/Chinese-inspired builds. **NOT when:** European builds
  (use T7's pane + shutter recipe); on dark walls (the pale panel needs a white
  or timber wall to contrast — on dark stone it inverts and reads as a hole).
- **Roast note (carried):** this is a T7 variant, not a new technique — same slot
  (window language per facade), different filling.
- (Dense ref: f0050 — timber-framed pale panels on the red hall; f0046/f0048 —
  timber-framed pale panels on the main hall's upper story `[v2: now confirmed
  on TWO buildings, not just the red hall]`; f0064 — shoji upper windows on the
  finished main hall.)

### 7. Forecourt garden — dirt court + round pond + bamboo/cherry framing (NEW) — MED-HIGH
- **Principle:** Distinct from candidate 4's formal tile-and-sand courtyard (a
  paved forecourt) and from T8's restrained zen bed (one bed, negative space).
  This is the planted forecourt in front of the main hall: a dirt court with a
  round pond as its centerpiece, framed by bamboo clusters and cherry trees —
  the garden is the arrival space, not a side feature.
- **Recipe:** In front of the main hall, lay a dirt court (coarse dirt / dirt
  path family) spanning the hall's width. Center a round pond: blue water, white
  stone rim, one small central ornament (white/stone feature). Frame the court
  with tall bamboo clusters (5–6 blocks, varied heights — T16 language) and 2–4
  cherry trees (pink canopies) at the court's edges, plus flower beds and dark
  lantern posts at intervals. Keep the court's center open — the pond owns it;
  all planting sits at the edges.
- **Apply when:** compounds with a main hall and a front garden, temple
  forecourts, estates with an arrival garden. **NOT when:** the forecourt is
  paved/formal (use candidate 4's tile-and-sand grid); tiny sites (the pond +
  bamboo need room — on a small plot the court chokes the hall).
- **Prevents:** the bare-dirt forecourt (an empty dirt patch in front of a grand
  hall reads as unfinished ground) and the pond-as-afterthought (an unrimmed
  water hole reads as a puddle, not a garden feature).
- **T8 relationship (flagged for roast):** T8 covers siting/gardens including
  "basin-first water" — the round pond may be a T8 extension rather than a
  standalone technique. Proposed standalone because the composition (dirt court
  + pond + bamboo/cherry frame as one arrival space) is a bigger unit than a
  basin; roast decides.
- (Dense ref: f0056–f0058 — dirt court + round pond + bamboo + cherry trees +
  lantern posts, garden complete; f0064 — pond + dirt court + bamboo + cherry
  trees from the gatehouse side; f0066–f0068 — forecourt garden in the finished
  showcase.)

### 8. Lit display-niche wall — grid of small glowing niches on an interior wall (T7 VARIANT — folds into T7, not standalone) — MED
- **Principle:** T7 covers detail & props; this is an interior wall treatment
  from the same slot: a flat wall covered in a regular grid of small lit niches
  (shelves/cubbies with warm light), turning a blank interior wall into a
  display surface.
- **Recipe:** On a main-hall interior wall, lay a regular grid of small niches
  (1-block cubbies, timber-framed) with a warm light in each — the grid reads as
  a lit display wall, not as windows (no glass, no exterior). Keep the grid
  regular and wall-sized; a partial grid reads as unfinished shelving.
- **Apply when:** large interior halls that need a focal wall without windows.
  **NOT when:** small rooms (the grid overwhelms); exterior walls (lit niches
  are an interior treatment — on an exterior they read as a strange window
  array).
- **Roast note:** proposed as a T7 interior variant, not a new technique — same
  slot (detail & props), different filling. Single-interior evidence (main hall
  only), hence MED.
- (Dense ref: f0031–f0032 — checkerboard grid of small glowing squares on the
  main-hall interior wall, with dining furniture below; f0040 — same wall from
  the upper-floor angle.)
