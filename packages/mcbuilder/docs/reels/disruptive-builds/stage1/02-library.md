## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: Grand library ref (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/02-library-ext.jpg` + `02-library-int.jpg`)

**Build observed:** two-story survival house/library on a hillside — dark timber-framed walls, cross-gabled light-plank roofs, stone accent tower. Interior per transcript: enchanting area immediately inside the entrance (the only functional room), mirrored bookshelf wings left/right, upper library floor, ladder-accessed private reading nook (the interior frame: bookshelf walls, plank table + stair chairs, lectern with open book, candle cluster, potted plant, hanging lantern, stone accent wall section, trapdoor wall paneling).

### 1. Lead with the function — entry-first functional core *(NEW — plan organization)*
- **Principle:** In a survival base, the player enters dozens of times a day. Putting the one functional room (enchanting, smelting, storage) immediately inside the door means purpose greets you first; the decorative rooms branch deeper. It makes the build read as a *base that happens to be beautiful*, not a museum you happen to sleep in.
- **Recipe:** Within 3–5 blocks of the main entrance, place the functional core: enchanting table ringed by bookshelves (15 for max level), anvil beside it, one lantern above. Keep this room compact (≤6×6) and its palette workmanlike (stone/planks, no display dressing). Corridors, stairs, or open archways *beyond* it lead to the decorative wings — never put a display room between the door and the function.
- **Apply when:** any survival base with ≥1 functional room and ≥1 decorative room. **NOT when:** pure display builds/dioramas with no function — then lead with the vista instead (technique 9's anchor logic).
- **Prevents:** the "beautiful but useless" base — grand rooms up front, enchanting table crammed in a closet at the back. (Library ref: enchanting area is the first thing through the front door; transcript: "that's the only thing useful in this entire build.")

### 2. Mirrored-wing plan *(NEW — plan-level symmetry)*
- **Principle:** For multi-room institutional builds (library, hall, temple), mirroring room pairs across the entry axis halves the design work and the symmetry itself reads as "grand" — bilateral symmetry is the cheapest monumentality there is.
- **Recipe:** Fix the entry axis (door centerline). Design wing A completely (room footprint, shelf/furniture layout, lighting). Build wing B as the mirror across the axis — then change 1–2 small details (a different potted plant, one swapped wall hanging) so it doesn't read as a clone stamp. Keep both wings' doorways equidistant from the entry and at the same height.
- **Apply when:** grand/institutional archetypes with ≥2 same-purpose rooms. **NOT when:** rustic/organic builds where asymmetry is the charm (cottages, farmhouses) — forced mirroring there reads as stiff.
- **Prevents:** lopsided multi-room builds where one side feels designed and the other feels leftover; designing every room from scratch. (Library ref: "on the left side here we have a nice little library area — same thing on the right side, it is just mirrored" — transcript-confirmed.)

### 3. Ladder nook — vertical access as a destination *(NEW — interior access; cousin of technique 7's exterior ladder bullet, different job)*
- **Principle:** Stairs say "second floor" (utility, frequent traffic). A ladder says "retreat" — the climb itself is a threshold, so the small room at the top reads as a secret nook rather than leftover attic. The access method *is* the room's identity.
- **Recipe:** From the main room, run a 1×1 ladder shaft (ladders on the wall face, 4–8 tall) to a small enclosed loft ≤5×5. Furnish it as a single-purpose retreat: one table, 2 chairs, bookshelves on 2–3 walls. Keep the loft ceiling 2–3 blocks (cozy, not a full story). Put exactly one deliberate detail up there (lectern + open book, or a flower pot + candle) so arrival feels rewarded.
- **Apply when:** a small special-purpose room (reading nook, lookout, shrine) above a larger room. **NOT when:** the upper space gets daily functional traffic (use stairs — ladders punish routine); when the loft is >5×5 (that's a second floor, give it stairs and call it one).
- **Prevents:** dead attic voids; ladders that terminate at nothing (technique 7's ladder rule, interior edition). (Library ref: ladder up to the private reading nook — transcript-confirmed; interior frame shows the furnished nook.)

### 4. Storage walls — the walls ARE the storage *(NEW — interior construction)*
- **Principle:** A themed room with freestanding storage furniture wastes its perimeter and reads cluttered. Building the walls themselves out of the storage block (bookshelves for a library, barrels for a cellar, chests behind trapdoor fronts for a vault) makes storage architectural — the room's identity and its function are the same blocks.
- **Recipe:** Line 2–3 walls of the room floor-to-ceiling with the themed storage block (bookshelves: 3–4 high). Cap the run with a plate/trim course (log or slab) at the top so it reads as intentional millwork, not a shelf avalanche. Reserve the remaining wall(s) for the door + ONE feature (window, lectern wall, fireplace). Leave a 1-block walkway clear — shelves to the wall face, never jutting into the room.
- **Apply when:** single-theme rooms (library, cellar, armory, pantry). **NOT when:** the room needs windows on multiple faces; when the storage block fights the room's palette (then use technique 7's recessed niches instead).
- **Prevents:** storage as afterthought clutter (chests scattered on floors); big empty rooms with one sad bookcase in the corner. (Library ref: the nook's walls are floor-to-ceiling bookshelves — interior frame.)

### 5. Trapdoor wall paneling *(NEW — interior material; one interpretive read, flagged)*
- **Principle:** Flat interior plank walls are as guilty as flat exterior walls (technique 1), but you can't always re-palette indoors. Cladding a feature wall section in closed vertical trapdoors gives a slatted-timber panel texture from a block every builder has — wainscoting for the cost of a crafting table.
- **Recipe:** Behind the room's feature (lectern, fireplace, bed headboard), clad a 2–4 wide × 2–3 tall section in closed trapdoors of a dark wood, placed flat against the wall face. Frame the panel with a 1-wide contrasting trim (stripped log or stair border). One paneled section per room — two competing panels chop the room up.
- **Apply when:** an interior feature wall needs texture without a palette change. **NOT when:** exterior walls (reads as weathering damage, not paneling); walls already carrying shelves or windows (one wall treatment at a time).
- **Prevents:** flat featureless interior walls behind the room's focal furniture. (Library ref: slatted dark panels flanking the stone accent section read as closed dark-oak trapdoors — *interpretive read*; if they are planks, the technique holds as "slatted panel cladding" — the construction is standard real-builder practice either way.)

### 6. Anchor every room with one functional centerpiece *(extends technique 7's interiors — the "fireplace as the room's anchor" bullet)*
- **Principle:** The interior board's anchor rule is stated for living rooms (fireplace). It generalizes: every themed room needs ONE functional block as its visual anchor, with furniture and light oriented to it — otherwise seating floats aimlessly and the room has no focal point.
- **Recipe:** Library → lectern with an open book (book + quill on top); enchanting room → enchanting table; smithy → anvil; kitchen → smoker/cauldron. Place the anchor centered on the feature wall or the room's middle; arrange seating facing it; put task light AT it (candle cluster or lantern directly beside/above — not across the room). One anchor per room — two anchors split the focus.
- **Apply when:** every themed interior room. **NOT when:** pure circulation spaces (hallways, stairwells) — they borrow focus from the rooms they connect.
- **Prevents:** furniture arranged against walls with a dead middle; rooms where the eye finds nothing to land on. (Library ref: lectern + open book centered in the nook, candle cluster beside it as task light — interior frame.)
