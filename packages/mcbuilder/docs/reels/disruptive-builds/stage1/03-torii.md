## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: torii zen garden (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/03-torii.jpg`)

**Build observed:** a zen garden vignette next to the torii — dense bamboo clusters at varied heights, a stepped stone pedestal with a lantern as focal point, irregular stepping-stone slabs across a pale sand bed, water with lily pads at the garden's edge, and a dark leaf canopy with hanging vines overhead enclosing the space. Real Minecraft build footage (not a render) — block reads are trustworthy; inferences flagged inline.

### 1. Stepping-stone crossings *(extends technique 5's "patchy paths")*
- **Principle:** A continuous path is a road; a stepping-stone crossing is a *moment*. Offset bottom-half slabs with 1-block gaps force a slower read and let the ground bed show through between stones — the path becomes part of the garden rather than a stripe across it.
- **Recipe:** Across the garden bed, lay bottom-half slabs (mix stone-brick slabs + andesite slabs + stone slabs, ~even thirds) spaced 1 block apart, each stone offset half a block left/right of the last — never a straight grid. Slabs sit 1 block below the bed surface level so they read as *set into* the ground, not laid on it. Crossing width: 1 stone wide for garden paths, 2 for main approaches.
- **Apply when:** crossing a contrasting ground bed (sand, gravel, moss) or shallow water; garden/teahouse/shrine grounds. **NOT when:** high-traffic main streets (reads fussy); crossing deep water (use a bridge — technique 9's transport lines).
- **Prevents:** the "dirt_path stamp" — a flat uniform path that ignores the ground it's on. (Torii ref: slab stones wandering across the pale sand bed, each offset.)

### 2. Bounded garden beds with contrasting ground *(extends technique 8's enclosed plots)*
- **Principle:** Technique 8's plots are formal and planted. The zen variant inverts it: the bed is mostly EMPTY — a pale sand/gravel rectangle does the visual work, and a handful of placed elements (one lantern pedestal, one bamboo cluster, a few stones) carry the composition. Negative space is the material.
- **Recipe:** Cut a bed 5–9 blocks across into the grass; fill with sand or gravel (one material, un-noised — the restraint is the point). Edge it with a 1-block border of the surrounding grass left ragged, OR a single course of stone slabs. Place exactly 3–5 elements: one vertical anchor (bamboo cluster, technique 3), one focal (lantern pedestal, technique 4), 1–3 low stones (cobblestone/stone, half-buried = bottom-half slab sunk 1 into the sand). Nothing else. If you want to add a sixth element, remove one first.
- **Apply when:** shrine/temple/teahouse grounds, town squares, courtyard corners. **NOT when:** the build's story is wilderness or farm (empty beds read as unfinished there — use technique 8's planted plots instead); more than one bed per ~15-block radius (they compete; one zen bed per grounds).
- **Prevents:** garden-as-clutter — ten props on grass with no composition; and the twin failure of paving the whole grounds in one material. (Torii ref: the pale sand bed holding only pedestal + bamboo + stones.)

### 3. Vertical bamboo screens — planting as walls *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** The guide has hedges (technique 5) as low edging. Bamboo does the opposite job: planted at varied heights (6–12 tall), it forms a translucent green WALL that encloses a space vertically without blocking light — an outdoor room with living walls.
- **Recipe:** Plant bamboo in clusters of 5–9 stalks on grass/dirt, stalks 1 block apart in an irregular line (never a straight row — stagger ±1). Let them grow to mixed heights (break the tops at 6, 8, 10, 12 — uniform height reads as a fence). Use the screen to back ONE side of a garden bed or path (technique 2's bed + technique 3's screen = the complete vignette). Bamboo only spreads on grass/dirt — contain it with a stone-slab border or it escapes the composition.
- **Apply when:** enclosing shrine/garden/teahouse spaces; screening an ugly boundary (a cliff face, a neighbor's wall). **NOT when:** the palette is arid/desert (bamboo reads wrong — use dead bushes/fences); small builds where a 10-tall screen dwarfs the architecture (screen height ≤ ~1.5× the adjacent wall height).
- **Prevents:** gardens that bleed into the landscape with no edge; flat backdrops behind focal builds. (Torii ref: the towering bamboo wall behind the lantern pedestal — the garden's entire backdrop.)

### 4. Lantern-on-pedestal garden focal *(extends technique 6's lighting rhythm)*
- **Principle:** Technique 6's lanterns are rhythmic repeats. The garden focal is the inverse move: ONE lantern, elevated on a stepped stone pedestal, placed as the single bright point the whole bed is composed around. Mass + light in one object — it works in daylight (stone mass) and at night (light).
- **Recipe:** Pedestal: 3×3 base of stone/andesite, 2×2 second course, 1 block cap (3 high total); set ONE lantern centered on the cap. Position it off-center in the bed (never dead center — centered reads as a maypole). Keep every other light in the grounds ≥8 blocks away so this one stays the focal point — one focal per garden, rhythm everywhere else.
- **Apply when:** the center of a bounded bed (technique 2), shrine approaches, courtyard corners. **NOT when:** the facade already runs a lantern rhythm on the same sightline (technique 6's guard: one system — focal XOR rhythm per view); modern/minimalist builds (use a glowstone-free flush light instead).
- **Prevents:** gardens with no eye-rest — the visitor's gaze slides off; and lantern spam where every bed gets a light and none of them mean anything. (Torii ref: the lantern on its stepped andesite pedestal, the bed's single focal point.)

### 5. Water edge detailing — pads, shore, steam *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** The guide barely covers water surfaces. Still water with nothing on it reads as a hole in the terrain; three cheap details finish it: lily pads on the surface, a ragged shoreline, and (per the video's narration) submerged campfires for a "steaming" effect.
- **Recipe:** On any still-water edge: (a) scatter 3–7 lily pads on the water within 2 blocks of the shore — clustered, never covering >30% of the surface; (b) ragged shore: replace the waterline blocks with a mix of dirt, gravel, and stone (no straight lines — nibble the edge ±1); (c) steaming water *(from the video narration, NOT visible in this frame — verify before trusting)*: place campfires 1 block under the water surface near the shore; the smoke particles rising through water read as steam. Unlit campfires also work as dark stones on the pond floor.
- **Apply when:** any pond, moat edge, or stream in garden/shrine grounds. **NOT when:** the water is meant to read as deep/mysterious (pads + steam make it shallow and domestic); ocean builds (wrong scale — pads read as debris at ocean scale).
- **Prevents:** the "blue hole" — flat water planes that look like missing terrain; and overbuilt ponds where the edging costs more than the garden. (Torii ref: lily pads on the pond behind the garden; ragged grass-to-water edge.)

### 6. Overhead canopy to enclose outdoor rooms *(extends technique 8's grounds-as-architecture)*
- **Principle:** Technique 8 composes grounds horizontally (plots, paths, hedges). The missing axis is vertical: a leaf canopy with hanging vines overhead turns an open garden into a ROOM — ceiling, walls (technique 3's bamboo), floor (technique 2's bed). Enclosure on three axes is what makes small grounds feel designed instead of decorated.
- **Recipe:** Grow 2–4 large custom trees (dark oak or jungle, 8–12 tall) around the garden so their canopies meet overhead, 6–9 blocks above the bed. Hang vines from the canopy edge (vines on the underside of leaf blocks, 2–4 long) — the dripping edge is what sells "ceiling" instead of "trees nearby." Keep the canopy OPEN directly above the focal (technique 4's lantern) so light still hits it — a skylight, not a lid. Prune any leaves below head height +2 on the approach side so visitors can enter.
- **Apply when:** shrine/forest garden rooms, courtyard corners, anywhere a garden needs intimacy. **NOT when:** the build needs sun on crops or solar theming below (canopy shades it out); arid builds (a jungle ceiling over a desert garden reads wrong); tiny beds where the trees dwarf everything (canopy span ≥2× the bed width or skip it).
- **Prevents:** gardens that feel like props on a lawn — no ceiling, no room, no intimacy. (Torii ref: the dark leaf mass with hanging vines roofing the whole garden shot.)

### 7. Texture the path, don't pave it *(extends technique 1's noise rule)*
- **Principle:** Technique 1's 30% noise rule is written for walls. Paths need it more: a 20-block run of pure stone bricks is the single most common "polosan" surface in survival builds. The fix is the same verb — mottled mix, ~30% secondary — applied to the ground plane.
- **Recipe:** Path base: stone bricks. Mix in ~20% stone and ~10% andesite (and cracked stone bricks near water/wear points), scattered with no pattern — place the variants as you walk the path, never in runs. Border the path with a 1-block grass/dirt ragged edge (technique 5's "sit in terrain"), never a hard slab curb — curbs make paths read as roads. *(Flag: the path itself isn't clearly visible in this frame — this recipe is from the video's narration of the torii approach; the frame shows the sand bed, not the path. Treat the mix ratio as provisional.)*
- **Apply when:** any path ≥5 blocks long. **NOT when:** the path is the formal axis of a symmetric build (technique 8's formal frames want clean geometry — use a single material there, deliberately); underwater (different palette rules).
- **Prevents:** the paved-runway look — paths that read as creative-mode fill commands. (Torii ref: narrated stone-brick/stone/andesite path mix — provisional, not frame-verified.)
