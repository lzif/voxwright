## Stage 1 output — 7 techniques from the gazebo frame

### 1. Gazebo vs pergola — the roofed-pavilion choice rule (NEW)
- **Principle:** Technique 12 covers the pergola (open, ~50% striped shade). A gazebo is its roofed cousin, and the guide has no rule for choosing between them. The choice is functional: a pergola shades a *passage or seat* and lets sun through; a gazebo is a *destination* — it needs a solid roof (weather, real shelter) and a floor (platform), because people stand in it, not under it.
- **Recipe:** Build a gazebo when the spot is a node (town center, garden focal point, viewpoint): solid stair-block roof + raised platform floor. Build a pergola when the spot is a route or lean-to seat: open slat roof, no raised floor needed.
- **Apply when:** the pavilion must read as a *room* (gazebo) vs as *shade* (pergola). **NOT when:** you need both on the same spot — a solid roof over a pergola's striped slats is two shade systems fighting (same guard as technique 12).
- **Prevents:** roofed gazebos built where a pergola would do (heavy, dark, overbuilt) and pergolas built where people need real shelter (useless in rain). (Gazebo ref)

### 2. Stepped plinth platform *(extends technique 5: ground connection)*
- **Principle:** Technique 5 says "foundation wider than the footprint, with steps up." On a pavilion the platform IS the architecture: 2–3 stair risers lift a 1-story open structure off the ground plane and give it the presence of a stage — without the platform it's four posts stuck in grass.
- **Recipe:** Platform of stone-brick family (slabs/stairs) extending 1–2 blocks past the post line on all sides. Rise 2–3 steps (≈1–1.5 blocks above grade): build the edge as stair blocks, not full blocks — the stepped profile is the detail. Put the steps on the approach side(s) only; other edges get a clean slab lip. Platform floor itself: bottom-half slabs or full blocks, one material, no noise needed at this size.
- **Apply when:** any freestanding pavilion, gazebo, or shrine. **NOT when:** the pavilion sits on an existing terrace/deck (don't stack platforms — one ground plane per structure).
- **Prevents:** the "posts in grass" read — a pavilion with no base looks temporary, like scaffolding. (Gazebo ref: 2-step stone stair front, slab-lipped platform)

### 3. Four-post minimal frame + eave plate *(extends technique 2: skeleton-first framing)*
- **Principle:** Technique 2's frame is written for walled buildings. A pavilion strips it to the minimum that still reads as structure: four proud corner posts + one full-perimeter plate beam under the eaves. The plate is the shadow line that says "roof sits on frame" — without it the roof floats.
- **Recipe:** 4 posts of stripped logs or dark-oak logs at the platform corners, 3–4 blocks tall, standing ON the platform (never on dirt — technique 5's footing rule). Cap with a full-perimeter plate course (logs laid horizontally or bottom-half slabs) on all 4 sides; roof structure bears on the plate. Keep joints exposed — post-to-plate connections are the decoration (technique 2's "exposed frame IS decoration").
- **Apply when:** pavilions with 3×3 to 5×5 post grids. **NOT when:** the span exceeds ~5×5 — beyond that the plate sags visually; add interior posts or a center king post, or it reads as a hall missing its columns.
- **Prevents:** roofless-looking post clusters; roofs that hover above posts with no visible bearing. (Gazebo ref: dark-oak corner posts, continuous eave plate)

### 4. Frieze band between posts *(extends technique 7: "parapet the terraces")*
- **Principle:** Technique 7's parapet turns "flat area outside the door" into a defined outdoor room. The same verb applies vertically: an open pavilion side with nothing between post and plate reads as *missing walls*. A lattice band spanning post-to-post just under the eaves fills the top of the opening — the side reads as designed openness, not an unfinished wall.
- **Recipe:** Between adjacent posts, just under the eave plate, run a 1-block-tall band of fences (dark oak fences, 1 high) spanning the full post-to-post gap — reads as a lattice frieze. Alternative for low railings: fence posts at waist height with a top-half slab handrail (technique 7's parapet grammar). Keep the band in the upper third of the opening so the pavilion stays walk-through at ground level.
- **Apply when:** any open-sided pavilion, carport, or market stall where the sides must read intentional. **NOT when:** the design wants a completely unobstructed view axis (viewpoint pavilions) — then use the band on 3 sides and leave the view side clean.
- **Prevents:** open sides reading as walls that were never built; the "abandoned frame" look. (Gazebo ref: fence lattice band under the eaves on all visible sides)

### 5. Pyramid stair roof at pavilion scale *(extends technique 4: silhouette discipline)*
- **Principle:** Technique 4's stair roofs are written for houses. At pavilion scale the same construction — stepped stair courses forming a hipped/pyramid roof — is what keeps a 5×5 roof from reading as a flat lid. The overhang is doing the silhouette work that a chimney or gable does on a house.
- **Recipe:** Hipped roof of stair blocks (dark oak or deepslate), each course stepping 1 block inward per 1 block of rise, overhanging the post line by ~1 block on all sides. Cap the apex with a slab + fence-post finial (or a lantern for a lit cap). Underside: leave the stair soffits exposed — no ceiling; the stepped underside is free texture.
- **Apply when:** square pavilions, gazebos, wellheads, guard posts. **NOT when:** the pavilion is rectangular beyond ~2:1 — then use a gable (ridge) roof instead; a stretched pyramid reads as a mistake.
- **Prevents:** the "four posts + flat lid" anti-pattern — the single most common small-pavilion failure. (Gazebo ref: stepped dark-oak pyramid roof, ~1-block overhang, slab cap)

### 6. Single pendant lantern as the pavilion's hearth *(extends technique 6: lighting rhythm)*
- **Principle:** Technique 6's pendant rows light tables; a pavilion has no table — it has one room. A single lantern hung from the roof's center beam becomes the visual anchor the way a fireplace anchors a room (technique 7 interiors): the eye goes to the warm point, and the posts read as gathered around it.
- **Recipe:** Hang ONE lantern from the underside of the roof's center beam (or from a short 1-block fence drop at the apex interior). Identical fixture language to the rest of the build. One per pavilion — two pendants in a 5×5 space read as clutter, not rhythm.
- **Apply when:** any roofed pavilion, gazebo, or wellhead. **NOT when:** the pavilion is unroofed (pergola) — hang the lantern from a post arm instead (technique 6's wall-mount variant logic).
- **Prevents:** a roofed pavilion that's pitch dark inside at night (reads as a cave); torches slapped on posts (technique 6's mob-proofing read). (Gazebo ref: single warm lantern hanging under the roof center)

### 7. Path-to-steps arrival for pavilions *(extends technique 11: stage the arrival)*
- **Principle:** Technique 11 stages the last blocks before a door. A pavilion has no door — the steps ARE the threshold. The arrival grammar compresses to: path → steps → platform. If the path doesn't meet the steps on axis, visitors arrive at the platform's side and the composition has no front.
- **Recipe:** Run a 2–3-wide slab path (stone-brick slabs, technique 5's path language) straight to the platform steps, on axis with the pavilion's center. Steps land ON the path — no grass gap. Keep the path's far end connected to the town's path network (technique 11's rule: props/paths with no network read as random).
- **Apply when:** every freestanding pavilion or gazebo. **NOT when:** the pavilion is a viewpoint reached by trail — then the trail may curve, but it must still arrive at the steps, not the back.
- **Prevents:** the "object on a lawn" read — a nicely built gazebo stranded with no way to walk to it. (Gazebo ref: light slab path running to the front steps, continuing past)
