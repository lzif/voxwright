Frame studied: first-person view from a plank pier over shader-lit water — two parallel plank fingers with a water gap between them (the boat slip), terraced shoreline behind. ### 1. Finger-slip boat mooring (NEW)
- **Principle:** A boat tied alongside a straight pier swings into the walkway and blocks it. Splitting the pier end into two parallel fingers with a water gap *between* them gives the boat its own parking bay — it noses into the slip and never touches foot traffic.
- **Recipe:** Two pier fingers, each 2 wide × ≥5 long, of planks on log piles, running parallel with a **3-wide water gap** between them (a boat is ~1.4 blocks wide; 1 block clearance each side). Cap the outer end of each finger with a fence post; put fender posts (fences, 2 high) at the two inner corners of the slip mouth so a mis-parked boat hits wood, not deck. Keep the slip open to the sky — no roof, no cross-beams above it.
- **Apply when:** any waterfront build that parks boats: docks, harbors, boathouses. **NOT when:** the water is <7 wide total (fingers + gap won't fit — use a side tie-up: boat parallel to one pier edge with fender posts instead); never roof the slip or bridge across it (boats need the vertical clearance, and a bridge turns the slip into a tunnel).
- **Prevents:** boats beached sideways across the walkway; the "tie it anywhere" look. (Ultimate fishing ref: the frame's two plank paths with water between them ARE the slip.)

### 2. Activity ledges off the walkway (NEW)
- **Principle:** A straight pier is a corridor — everyone walks the same line and every activity (fishing, mooring, looking) fights for the edge. Small bumps protruding off the walkway edge, each dedicated to ONE verb, turn the corridor into a sequence of places.
- **Recipe:** Bump of planks **2 out × 2 wide** from the pier edge. Cut a gap in the pier railing exactly at the bump (the bump IS the opening); railing continues uninterrupted past it on both sides. One verb per ledge, named by its furnishing: fishing ledge (bare — stand and cast), mooring ledge (cleat/fence post), lookout bump (no furnishing, just the view). Space ledges 4–6 blocks apart along the pier.
- **Apply when:** piers, bridges, wall walks — any long walkway. **NOT when:** on the boat-slip side of a finger (fishing lines vs hulls); more than one furnishing per 2×2 ledge (it becomes clutter, not a place); ledges wider than 3 (that's a platform — use technique 7's terrace rules instead).
- **Prevents:** the "everyone fishes off the same corner" scrum; railings with no reason to stop. (Ultimate fishing ref: "we just stand on one of these little ledges and fish off" — transcript.)

### 3. Dress the dead space under the deck (NEW)
- **Principle:** A pier on bare posts has a visible black under-volume — from any low angle the build reads as a floating lid. One deliberate vignette tucked under the deck (visible from the approach) converts dead space into story.
- **Recipe:** Under-deck clearance ≥2 blocks. In ONE bay (between two pile pairs), cluster: 1 beached rowboat (a boat entity pulled half onto a dirt/gravel patch, or a block-built hull of stairs), 2–4 barrels/crates tight against the piles, and one rope coil (fence post + tripwire hook, or a barrel on its side). Light it with one lantern hung from a deck beam so the vignette reads at dusk. Keep the cluster clear of the slip lane.
- **Apply when:** any pier, bridge, or stilted structure with visible under-volume. **NOT when:** the under-deck is the boat lane (never block navigation); more than one vignette per structure (scattered under-deck props read as junk, not story); on piers <6 long (there's no "under" to speak of).
- **Prevents:** the hollow-pier read — deck floating on naked posts. (Ultimate fishing ref: "under here we have a nice couple of decorations, we got a little boat here, some barrel and random stuff" — transcript.)

### 4. Sculptural machine as pure silhouette (extends technique 4)
- **Principle:** Technique 4 lists "a crane" as a vertical accent but never gives the construction or the permission structure. The dock's crane "does not serve function whatsoever" — and that's the point: a non-functional machine is legitimate as *pure sculpture* if it's themed and solitary. It gives flat waterfront builds a skyline for the cost of ~30 blocks.
- **Recipe:** Tower: 5–7 high of log pillars (2×2 or 1×1 with cross-bracing fences). Jib: 4–6 long arm from the top, stepped upward via stairs at ~30°, built of planks/logs. Counter-jib: 2 back with a crate (barrel/chest) as counterweight. Cable: 2–3 chain or fence links hanging from the jib tip with a tripwire hook. Set it back from the pier edge so the jib overhangs water, not the walkway.
- **Apply when:** waterfronts, quarries (technique 9's industrial zones), docks, shipyards — anywhere the theme is work. **NOT when:** the build already has a technique-10 anchor or a belvedere (one skyline strategy per build — the crane IS the anchor here); more than one machine (two cranes = construction site, not sculpture); on builds whose story isn't labor (a crane on a pleasure garden reads as a mistake).
- **Prevents:** flat waterfront skylines; the "why is there a crane" objection — theme answers it, function doesn't need to. (Ultimate fishing ref: "We have a crane here as well which of course does not serve function whatsoever" — transcript.)

### 5. Vertical live/work split on a pier hut (NEW)
- **Principle:** A waterside hut that mixes bed, furnaces, and anvil in one room reads as a storage shed someone sleeps in. Splitting by floor — noisy work at deck level, quiet living above, ladder between — gives a 5×5 hut the zoning of a house twice its size, and puts the bedroom where the view is.
- **Recipe:** Hut 5×5 or 5×6 on/adjacent to the pier. **Ground floor (work):** furnace bank (3 furnaces in a row), anvil, crafting table, double chests along the walls; door facing the pier; no bed down here. **Ladder:** 2×1 opening or wall ladder to the upper floor. **Upper floor (live):** bed + 1–2 chests + a window or balcony door on the water side; **balcony** off the bedroom built as technique 7's indivisible unit (slab floor + brackets/posts + railing). Work functions that serve boats (smelting, storage) stay at deck level — never make the player climb stairs carrying cargo.
- **Apply when:** waterside huts, gatehouses with quarters, workshop-residences. **NOT when:** single-function sheds (a pure boathouse doesn't need a bedroom); at ≥3 stories the stacking discipline (technique 13) takes over — this is the ≤2-story version; don't put the bedroom at deck level "for convenience" (that's what kills the zoning).
- **Prevents:** the bed-next-to-furnaces shed; the dead upper floor (ladder to an empty attic). (Ultimate fishing ref: "we have our smelting area, a little bit of storage and an anvil… heading up the ladder, we have our bedroom… this door leads us out to our balcony" — transcript.)

### 6. Pier piling rhythm with cross-bracing (extends technique 5)
- **Principle:** Technique 5 demands visible supports and caps undercroft post spacing at ≤3, but piers need their own rhythm: piles at regular intervals with X-bracing between them read as *engineered*, while random piles read as afterthought stilts.
- **Recipe:** Log piles (spruce/dark oak logs) under both pier edges every **3 blocks**, driven from deck to waterbed — no floating. Between consecutive piles on the outer faces, run diagonal fence rails forming X-braces (fence zigzag). Keep bracing FLUSH with the pile faces on slip sides — nothing protrudes into the boat gap. Cap pile tops with the deck beams (technique 2's plate logic: beam course at deck line, piles standing under it).
- **Apply when:** any pier, jetty, or boardwalk over water. **NOT when:** water is ≤2 deep (piles barely show — use a stone footing instead); bracing across the slip mouth (boats hit it); pile spacing >4 (the deck visibly sags between supports).
- **Prevents:** floating-deck syndrome; the "toothpick stilts" read. (Ultimate fishing ref: pier fingers over open water — frame.)

### 7. Linear waterfront arrival (extends technique 11)
- **Principle:** Technique 11 stages the last few blocks before a door; a pier stretches that sequence over water into a procession: land → walk → work → dwell. Each zone transition is a beat, and the base door sits at the END so the entire pier is the approach, not an appendage.
- **Recipe:** Order zones along one axis from shore: **(a)** land entrance (path meets pier — technique 11's outer marker: posts/gate), **(b)** the walk with activity ledges (technique 2 of this set) as beats, **(c)** the boat slip + work props (crates, crane — the labor zone), **(d)** the hut door at the far end. Mark each transition: a widened section, a pair of lantern posts, or a material change in the deck. Keep the walk itself ≤2 wide so it reads as a path with destinations, not a platform with clutter.
- **Apply when:** piers, causeways, bridge-towns — any linear waterfront build. **NOT when:** the hut sits at the LAND end (then the pier is a dead appendage — move the door or accept a fishing-only pier); on piers <10 long (there's no room for beats — use plain technique 11 at the hut door instead).
- **Prevents:** the tacked-on dock — a pier with no procession that reads as an afterthought rather than the build's spine. (Ultimate fishing ref: "the entrance for this one is over here on land… that leads us over to our giant pier… and then that leads us to our actual base area" — transcript.)
