## The techniques (7)

### 1. Boat slip cut into the pier (NEW)
- **Principle:** A boat moored *alongside* a flat pier edge reads as parked-afterthought, and boarding is an awkward jump. Cutting a slip *into* the pier footprint makes the boat part of the composition — deck on both sides, step-across boarding, and the boat is visually "stored" rather than abandoned.
- **Recipe:** Pier deck ≥3 wide. Carve a 2-wide, 3–4-long notch into one deck edge (a boat is ~1.4 wide; 2 gives clearance). Line the notch walls with the deck material down to 1 block above water; water surface 1 below deck level so the boat floats with its gunwale near deck height. Put the slip mid-pier, not at the far end — the far end is the fishing platform (technique 4).
- **Apply when:** any dock where boats are actually used. **NOT when:** purely decorative piers, or piers narrower than 3 (no room — moor alongside instead).
- **Prevents:** boats bobbing beside the pier; the "where do I get in" moment. (Fishing dock ref: player's boat sits in the notch between the main pier arm and the lower dock section.)

### 2. Pile rhythm under the deck (extends technique 5: visible supports)
- **Principle:** Technique 5 says raised structures need visible supports; on water the support *is* the decoration. A regular pile rhythm with a beam course at deck level sells "built over water" the way a foundation sells "built on land."
- **Recipe:** Under the deck edges, drive log piles (stripped logs, or fence posts for slim reads) from deck underside down 2–3 blocks into the water, spaced every 3–4 blocks along both long sides, always in pairs across the deck width. At deck level run a horizontal beam course (logs laid sideways) connecting each pile pair — the beam is the shadow line that reads as engineering. Piles must reach *into* the water, never stop at the surface.
- **Apply when:** any pier, jetty, or over-water walkway. **NOT when:** shallow ornamental ponds (use stone edging instead — piles in 1-deep water read as fence posts in a puddle).
- **Prevents:** floating-deck syndrome — the #1 reason piers look wrong. (Fishing dock ref: deck clearly spans open water; pile construction inferred — standard real-builder practice, piles not directly visible in this frame.)

### 3. Service-counter hut front (extends technique 7: trapdoor cabinet doors)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's trapdoor cabinet doors are interior dressing. Turned outward, they become architecture: the hut's storage wall faces the pier as a *counter*, so the hut "serves" the dock instead of being a blank box beside it.
- **Recipe:** On the hut face toward the pier: a row of chests/barrels at counter height (y+1 above deck), fronted with closed vertical trapdoors; a bottom-half slab counter top overhanging the trapdoor face by 1 block; above it a serving-hatch window (1–2 wide opening with open trapdoors as shutters, per technique 7's shutter recipe). Wood tone matched to the pier deck.
- **Apply when:** dock huts, market stalls, workshop fronts — anywhere storage should be reachable without entering. **NOT when:** the hut interior is the point (then give it a door, not a counter); never counter on all four faces (pick the working face).
- **Prevents:** hut as dead box; storage buried inside where the pier's story can't use it. (Fishing dock ref: hut front shows trapdoor-fronted compartments at counter height with a small through-window above.)

### 4. Linear station sequencing (extends technique 11: stage the arrival, + technique 9: label zones)
- **Principle:** A pier is a line, and a walk out over water is a journey — give it beats. Technique 11 stages the last blocks before a door; on a pier the whole deck is the approach, so zone it as stations: shore (threshold) → service (hut) → activity (open platform at the far end).
- **Recipe:** Keep the shore-end 2–3 blocks clear as the threshold (technique 11's outer marker works here: posts flanking the pier root). Place the hut about 1/3 out. Reserve the far 1/3 as open platform — no structures, clear casting edges (technique 6). Each station gets its lantern (technique 7 below).
- **Apply when:** piers ≥10 long. **NOT when:** short piers <8 (not enough length for stations — one hut max, everything else stays clear).
- **Prevents:** pier as featureless plank runway; hut crammed at the far end blocking the fishing spot. (Fishing dock ref: pier → hut mid-span → open water beyond; matches the build's described layout of hut, then platform.)

### 5. Waterline weathering band (extends technique 1: material variation)
- **Principle:** Technique 1's noise rule, localized: water stains the base. A clean, uniform waterline reads as "just placed"; a mossy/darkened band at the splash zone reads as weathered and grounds the structure in the water.
- **Recipe:** On every pile and any stone at the waterline, swap the bottom 1–2 blocks (the splash zone: water surface ±1) to mossy/water-darkened variants — mossy cobblestone on stone, dark oak on oak piles — at ~50% mix right at the waterline, fading to clean above. Keep it tight: the band is 2 blocks tall max.
- **Apply when:** any structure touching water. **NOT when:** above the splash zone (mossy blocks 5 up a pile read as the wrong biome, not weathering).
- **Prevents:** the freshly-placed look on waterfront builds. (Fishing dock ref: construction inferred — waterline detail not resolvable in this frame; validated against standard practice.)

### 6. Open casting edges, railed walkways (extends technique 7: parapet the terraces)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's parapet defines outdoor rooms; on a pier the railing must serve *transit* without blocking *function*. Rail the walkway sides, leave the working end open.
- **Recipe:** Fence-post railing (posts + top-half slab cap, per the parapet bullet) along both long sides of the pier walk. At the terminal fishing platform, DROP the railing on the casting side(s) — finish with a contrasting slab edge instead. Keep railing on the approach side of the platform so the walk still feels edged.
- **Apply when:** working piers (fishing, diving, boat loading). **NOT when:** swimming/leisure docks where falling in is the point — or keep full rails and accept the look.
- **Prevents:** railing blocking the cast; or no railing making the walk feel like a plank to nowhere. (Fishing dock ref: deck edges near the boat read open — functional inference, consistent with the frame.)

### 7. Lantern posts at pier nodes (extends technique 6: lighting rhythm)
- **Principle:** Technique 6's fixture rhythm, applied to the pier's stations: lights mark the nodes — shore junction, hut corner, platform end — so the pier reads as a designed sequence at night, not a dark plank.
- **Recipe:** One identical fixture per node: fence post + lantern on top, placed at (a) the pier root where it meets shore, (b) beside the hut door, (c) one corner of the far platform. Same fixture everywhere, no extras.
- **Apply when:** any pier used at night. **NOT when:** on every pile (technique 6's cactus rule — a lantern every 3 blocks is a runway, not a rhythm).
- **Prevents:** dark dead pier at night; torches scattered on the deck (mob-proofing look). (Fishing dock ref: placement inferred from technique 6's rhythm rule applied to the station layout; standard practice.)
