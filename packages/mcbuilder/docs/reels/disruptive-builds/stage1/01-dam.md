## Stage 1 output: 7 techniques from the dam reference (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/01-dam.jpg`)

### 1. Water-level gradient weathering *(extends technique 1)*
- **Principle:** Technique 1's banding is driven by stories; this is banding driven by *environment*. Weathering follows a physical cause — the wettest zone gets the most weathered material — so the wall tells you where the water lives even when the water isn't visible.
- **Recipe:** Stack 3–4 horizontal material bands by wetness, wettest at the bottom: at/below the waterline = **moss blocks** (or mossy cobblestone); mid zone = **mossy stone bricks**; splash line = cracked stone bricks mixed ~50/50 with the clean base; above = clean **stone bricks**. Each band 2–4 blocks tall with slightly irregular edges (1-block teeth up/down, never a ruler-straight line). The gradient must be monotonic — wettest material at the bottom, always.
- **Apply when:** any structure touching water — dams, docks, bridge piers, sewers, wells, canals, fountains. **NOT when:** dry inland builds — weathering with no cause reads as random noise and breaks technique 1's logic; also not on builds meant to read as newly built.
- **Prevents:** water-adjacent builds reading as freshly placed blocks that ignore their environment. (Dam ref: moss → mossy cobble → mossy stone brick → stone brick up the dam face, confirmed in the reel transcript.)

### 2. Working openings — show the structure doing its job *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** A dam that reads as a solid wall is a failed dam. Vertical slots through the wall with water visibly moving through them turn a decorative barrier into a *working* machine — the opening is the proof of function.
- **Recipe:** Cut 2–3-wide vertical slots through the wall at water level, spaced 3–5 blocks apart. Set **iron bars** (or fence gates for a chunkier read — exact grate block slightly inferred from the frame) as a grate across each slot mouth. Let water visibly flow through — the flow, not the hole, sells it. Flank each slot with slightly darker jamb blocks (mossy variants) so the openings read as intentional, not damaged.
- **Apply when:** dams, millraces, waterwheel channels, sewer outflows, forge chimneys (smoke instead of water), any "machine" build. **NOT when:** the structure's story is ruin/abandonment (working openings imply maintenance); not on purely decorative walls — a slot with nothing flowing through it reads as a hole.
- **Prevents:** functional infrastructure reading as a solid decorative wall. (Dam ref: 4 sluice slots with grates, water trickling through to the lower basin.)

### 3. Symmetric bookend towers *(extends technique 4's pairing logic)*
- **Principle:** Technique 4 pairs chimneys ("two flanking the ridge read as manor"). Same verb at infrastructure scale: two towers bookending a long horizontal span give it a beginning and an end — the span reads as *spanned*, not endless.
- **Recipe:** At each end of the span (dam, bridge, wall, gate), build a 3×3 to 5×5 tower rising 1.5–2× the span's height above its deck, in the span's base material. Cap each tower distinctly from the span — crenellation, a cap course, or a lantern. Keep the pair identical; asymmetry here reads as unfinished, not designed.
- **Apply when:** any horizontal structure ≥10 blocks long needing termination — dams, bridges, curtain walls, gate passages. **NOT when:** the span is short (<10 blocks — the towers dwarf it and it becomes three towers); not when the span already terminates into terrain on both ends (then use technique 5 below).
- **Prevents:** long spans reading as endless or arbitrarily cut off. (Dam ref: twin small towers flanking the dam, confirmed in the reel transcript.)

### 4. Crown as circulation *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** The top of an infrastructure build is dead space unless you walk on it. Making the crown a railed walkway gives the player a reason to climb the structure — and a railed deck line is free silhouette detail (pairs with technique 4).
- **Recipe:** Finish the top as a walkable deck: **planks or slabs** for the walking surface, full-length **fence-post railing** with a cap rail (top-half slab or sideways log) on every exposed edge, and access **stairs** at minimum one end (a ladder on the face works for narrow dams). Deck width ≥3 blocks — narrower and the railing eats the walkway.
- **Apply when:** dams, city walls, aqueducts, bridges — any linear structure with a flat top. **NOT when:** the top is a roof (technique 4 territory); not on spans <3 wide (no room for walk + rail — use a simple parapet per technique 7 instead).
- **Prevents:** "what's up there?" dead crowns; unusable-looking infrastructure. (Dam ref: wooden bridge deck running the dam's full length on top.)

### 5. Terrain-stitched abutments *(extends techniques 5 + 8)*
- **Principle:** Where a dam/bridge/wall meets the hillside, a hard vertical joint reads as pasted-on. Stepping the structure *down* while the terrain rises to meet it dissolves the joint — the build grows out of the land instead of being glued to it.
- **Recipe:** At each end, step the wall down 1 block per 2–3 blocks of horizontal run as the bank rises. Cap the joint zone with **grass blocks** (not stone) and plant **grass tufts/ferns** along the seam — the vegetation is what sells "grown over" vs "butted against". Let the terrain do half the work: build into a slope rather than burying the joint in extra wall.
- **Apply when:** dams, bridges, walls, or roads meeting a hillside or bank. **NOT when:** freestanding structures on flat ground (no bank to stitch into); not when the design wants a crisp engineered abutment (formal canal locks — then detail the joint instead of hiding it).
- **Prevents:** hard abutment lines that read as pasted-on geometry. (Dam ref: dam flanks stepping into terraced grassy banks on both sides.)

### 6. Hanging vegetation on vertical faces *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** The guide hides the wall-meets-terrain seam with hedges (technique 5), but tall faces have no seam to hide — they need texture *on* the face. Draped vines/leaves break up large vertical stone areas and, crucially, they read as *age*: vegetation grows on old walls, never new ones.
- **Recipe:** Hang **vines** (or leaf blocks for a chunkier, more visible read) down from the top edge in strands 2–5 blocks long, varying the lengths. Cluster strands near corners, openings, and the wettest zone; keep ~60% of the face clear — the wall must still read as a wall. Never a uniform curtain.
- **Apply when:** old/damp stone structures — dams, ruins, wells, sewers, retaining walls. **NOT when:** new or formal builds (vegetation = age; on a fresh build it reads as a mistake); never full coverage (that's a bush, not a wall).
- **Prevents:** the "just built" read on structures that should feel old; flat vertical stone with no detail budget. (Dam ref: leaf/vine strands draped over the dam face, densest near the sluice openings.)

### 7. Armored outflow apron *(NEW)*
- **Principle:** Where water slams out of a structure, grass or dirt reads as "nature hasn't noticed the engineering." A paved apron of hard material at the impact zone says the builders knew exactly where the water goes.
- **Recipe:** In front of each outflow (sluice, spillway, waterfall base), lay a 3–5-block apron of **stone slabs** (slightly darker than the wall — deepslate or dark stone reads as wet), extending downstream. Feather **mossy variants** at the apron's far edge where it meets natural ground. Keep the apron thin (slabs, not full blocks) so it reads as paving, not a second wall.
- **Apply when:** dam sluices, spillways, mill outflows, fountain basins, any engineered water exit. **NOT when:** the water is meant to read as natural (a stream through a meadow — paving it kills the read).
- **Prevents:** engineered outflows dumping onto grass and reading as accidental. (Dam ref: stone-paved basin floor in front of the sluice slots.)
