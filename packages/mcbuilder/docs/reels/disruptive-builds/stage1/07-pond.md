## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: Pond ref (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/07-pond.jpg`)

**Build observed:** a town-center pond from the disruptive-builds reel ("14+ Build Projects for Survival Minecraft 1.19") — small water body with a continuous stone/stone-brick rim, wooden plank bridges crossing it, lantern-on-fence-post fixtures at intervals around the rim, a stepped stone entry descending into the water on one side, a stone-slab path feeding in, and a small roofed timber shelter at the pond's edge near a bridge landing. Real Minecraft build video (not a render) — block reads are trustworthy; the shelter's exact construction is flagged interpretive (partially visible in a single frame).

### 1. Stone-rimmed pond edge with stepped entry (NEW)
- **Principle:** A water body's edge is where it reads as designed or as a random hole. A continuous hard rim gives the pond a finished boundary; one stepped section turns the edge from a barrier into an invitation.
- **Recipe:** Ring the entire water perimeter with stone bricks, 1 block wide at water level; cap the ring with a contrasting lip of stone top-half slabs. On ONE section (2–3 wide), step the rim down into the water as stone-brick stairs — a "beach" entry, 2–3 steps deep. The rim sits exactly at water level: no grass or dirt touching the waterline anywhere else.
- **Apply when:** any designed water feature (ponds, canals, fountain basins, moats). **NOT when:** wild/natural ponds — there the soft grass-to-water transition is the point; a stone rim would read as a swimming pool in a forest.
- **Prevents:** the "random water hole" read; grass blocks eroding into the water; players unable to tell where the feature starts. (Pond ref: full stone-brick rim with slab lip; stepped entry visible on the right side descending into the water.)

### 2. Bridges: deck, rails, and landings (NEW)
- **Principle:** A bridge is three parts and all three are mandatory — deck alone is a gangplank, deck + rails without landings is a pier to nowhere. The landings are what make it a *bridge* (a crossing) rather than a *dock* (a destination).
- **Recipe:** Deck: planks (oak family), 2–3 wide, spanning the water at exactly 1 block above the water surface. Rails: fence posts on both deck edges with a top-half slab handrail, full span, no gaps. Landings: extend the deck 2 blocks past the water's edge on BOTH sides, and run the arriving path material *under* the landing (the path doesn't stop at the water — it continues beneath the bridge). Bridge must connect path to path.
- **Apply when:** any water crossing on a path network. **NOT when:** the far side has no path — then it's a fishing pier/dock, a different build (deck + rails still apply, landings don't).
- **Prevents:** plank strips floating over water reading as scaffolding; bridges that dead-end at the waterline; rail-less crossings that read as unfinished. (Pond ref: wooden plank bridge with fence railings crossing the pond; transcript confirms "a bridge leading both ways.")

### 3. Plaza as path hub (extends technique 8)
- **Principle:** Technique 8 lays out grounds like architecture; the pond is the town-scale application: paths need a center to radiate from, and a water plaza is the most legible center a town can have. The pond isn't decoration *beside* the paths — it *is* the path junction.
- **Recipe:** Place the water feature exactly where 3–4 paths meet. Pave a 2–3-block ring around the stone rim in slabs (the plaza floor — distinct from both the rim and the paths). Branch each path outward from the plaza ring, not from the water's edge. Size the water to the path count: ~5–7 wide for a 4-path town center; smaller for fewer.
- **Apply when:** towns/villages with ≥3 converging paths. **NOT when:** only one path passes by — then it's a wayside pond (techniques 1–2 only); forcing radial paths to nowhere reads as a roundabout with no exits.
- **Prevents:** path networks that wander with no center; town layouts that read as scattered buildings; ponds marooned beside a single path. (Pond ref: transcript — "you'd put it in the center of your town, all the pathways leading out would continue going.")

### 4. Water-rim lantern rhythm (extends technique 6)
- **Principle:** Technique 6's rhythm rule was written for structures; water rims need it more — at night an unlit pond edge is invisible (and a fall hazard read), while scattered torches around it read as mob-proofing. Identical fixtures on the rim's rhythm light the water and draw the plaza's outline after dark.
- **Recipe:** Lantern on top of a fence post, placed just outside the stone rim (on the grass/plaza side), spaced every 4–6 blocks around the full perimeter. One fixture type only — no mixing lanterns, torches, and glowstone. Keep fixtures clear of bridge landings (don't block the crossing).
- **Apply when:** any designed water feature near paths or builds. **NOT when:** wild ponds far from activity — lighting the wilderness reads as a runway.
- **Prevents:** the pond vanishing at night; torch-salad around the waterline. (Pond ref: lantern-topped posts visible at intervals around the rim — left edge, center, and right side.)

### 5. Roofed shelter at the path node (NEW)
- **Principle:** A path junction with water is a natural pause point; a small open shelter turns the pause into a *place*. Four posts and a solid roof are enough — it's a comma in the path's sentence, not a new room.
- **Recipe:** 4 log/fence posts (3 tall) in a 3×3 or 4×4 footprint at the pond's edge, beside (not on) a bridge landing. Cap with a simple solid roof: stair-block hip/gable roof with 1-block overhang, or a flat slab roof with fascia. Keep ALL sides open — no walls, no infill. Solid roof, not pergola slats (technique 12): the story here is rain shelter at a rest stop, not striped shade.
- **Apply when:** path nodes with a reason to linger (ponds, viewpoints, crossroads). **NOT when:** every junction gets one — one shelter per plaza max, or the path reads as a colonnade; never roof the bridge itself (dark tunnel over water reads as a sewer).
- **Prevents:** path nodes that are pure transit with nowhere to stand; shelters that accidentally become huts (walls = a different building). (Pond ref: small timber post-and-roof structure at the pond's edge by the bridge landing — construction partially visible; roof form interpretive from this single frame.)

### 6. Plant the rim, spare the water (NEW)
- **Principle:** A plaza pond has two planting zones and they obey opposite rules. Lush rim + clear water reads as "tended"; bare rim reads as "construction hole"; choked water reads as "swamp." The discipline is knowing which side of the stone rim each plant belongs on.
- **Recipe:** OUTSIDE the rim (grass, 1–3 blocks out): grass tufts, ferns, flowers, poppies/daisies mixed — dense is fine. INSIDE the water: at most 1–2 lily pads, nothing else. Never reeds/sugar cane in a plaza pond (they read as wild/untended); never let vegetation touch the water surface except the lily pads.
- **Apply when:** designed/founded water features (plazas, courtyards, estates). **NOT when:** the brief is a wild pond, bog, or jungle water — there, invert it: choke the water, soften the rim.
- **Prevents:** the two classic pond failures — the sterile bathtub (bare rim, empty water) and the swamp (vegetation winning). (Pond ref: flower/tuft planting on the grass around the rim; water surface clear.)
