### 1. Post-and-beam well frame with suspended bucket (NEW)
- **Principle:** A well reads as *working* — not decorative — when three parts form one vertical story: the frame (posts + beam), the drop line (chain), and the load (bucket) hanging over the shaft. Remove any one and it degrades: no chain = a pergola; no bucket = an unfinished frame; no shaft below = a gallows. The composition is the technique, and it transfers to any hoist grammar (cranes, gallows, mine headframes).
- **Recipe:** Two 1×1 posts, 4–5 blocks apart, each: 1 stone footing block (stone bricks/cobble) + 3 stripped-log posts above it (dark oak family — species read from footage). Crossbeam: 5–7 planks spanning post-to-post at the top, sitting ON the posts (not floating beside them), ends finished per technique 5 below. From the beam's exact center, hang 2–4 iron chain blocks (iron bars read the same at distance — flagged: chain vs bars not fully resolvable in frame). Bucket: one barrel hung at the chain's end, 1–2 blocks above the shaft mouth.
- **Apply when:** wells, cranes, gallows, mine headframes — any structure whose job is lifting. **NOT when:** the frame is purely decorative (then it's a pergola — technique 12 — and the chain is a lie).
- **Prevents:** the "decorative frame" failure — two posts and a beam with nothing hanging reads as an unfinished pergola, not a well. (Well ref)

### 2. Stone-collared post footings (extends technique 5; extends technique 7's porch-post footings)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's lean-to awning already puts porch posts on 1×1 stone foundation blocks; this is the same verb on a freestanding structure. Every wooden post starts on a stone block (stone bricks or cobble, 1×1×1), never wood-on-dirt and never wood-on-wood-platform. It draws the rot line where the eye expects weathering, and gives the light timber frame visual weight at the ground.
- **Recipe:** Under each post, one stone-brick/cobblestone block at grade, post rising directly from it. On a plinth (technique 3), the footing sits on the plinth — stone-on-stone is fine, the rule is about the wood never touching earth or decking.
- **Apply when:** any freestanding wooden post outdoors. **NOT when:** the post is part of a walled structure sitting on a full foundation (technique 5's foundation course already does the job).
- **Prevents:** posts that look planted like fence posts — thin, temporary, pullable. (Well ref: gray stone collars visible at both post bases.)

### 3. Civic plinth platform (extends technique 5 ground connection)
- **Principle:** Technique 5's "foundation wider than the footprint" for houses becomes, for civic/utility structures, a full plinth: a 1-block-raised stone platform extending 1–2 blocks past the frame on ALL sides. It lifts the structure out of the grass, gives 360° standing room (wells are used from every side), and marks "this is a place, not an object."
- **Recipe:** Stone slabs (or smooth stone — flagged: slab vs full block not fully resolvable), footprint + 2 on each axis, top 1 block above grade. Edge the platform with a contrasting slab border if the build can afford it; keep it plain if not — the lift is the point, not the trim.
- **Apply when:** wells, fountains, statues, bell towers, market stalls — any small civic structure. **NOT when:** the structure is meant to look makeshift or hidden (then ground it directly, deliberately).
- **Prevents:** the "dropped on grass" read — a well sitting directly on turf looks like a prop someone forgot, not town infrastructure. (Well ref: light-gray square platform under the whole frame.)

### 4. Suspend the trade's tool (NEW)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's "bits" lists chains as dressing; this is the composed version: hang the structure's *working implement* from its frame so the build implies motion and function. A bucket on a chain says "this well draws water" the way a hook says "this crane lifts" and a rope says "this gallows hangs." The suspended tool is the cheapest possible storytelling — one chain + one block — and it's what separates equipment from sculpture.
- **Recipe:** Chain: 2–4 iron chain blocks (or iron bars) hung from the frame's load point, perfectly vertical. Implement at the end: barrel for wells, tripwire hook or fence-gate "hook" for cranes, lead-colored rope (string/fence) for gallows. Position it over the work point — shaft center, not frame center, if those differ.
- **Apply when:** any structure with a job: wells, cranes, gallows, fishing davits, mine headframes. **NOT when:** there is no plausible work (a second hanging thing on a decorative frame reads as a chandelier — exactly ONE suspended implement per structure).
- **Prevents:** utility structures that read as abstract sculpture — the frame is right but nothing about it says what it *does*. (Well ref: barrel-bucket on chain, centered over the shaft.)

### 5. Finished beam ends (extends technique 4's finished-edge rule, downscaled)
- **Principle:** Technique 4 makes a finished edge MANDATORY on small flat roofs; the same law holds at beam scale. A crossbeam cut off flat at both ends reads as an uncut plank. Stepping the ends out with upside-down stairs (or capping with slabs) gives the beam a profile — it's the difference between "placed" and "built."
- **Recipe:** At each beam end, add one upside-down stair of the beam's wood stepping outward 1 block (or a half-slab cap). Keep it to 1 block — longer reads as antlers, per technique 4's 1-block-cap logic on rafter tails.
- **Apply when:** any exposed beam end: well crossbeams, pergola beams (technique 12), sign posts, gallows arms. **NOT when:** the beam end is buried in a joint or wall (no visible end, no finish needed).
- **Prevents:** the "lumber pile" read — raw cut ends that make a finished frame look like stacked materials. (Well ref: stepped stair ends visible on both sides of the crossbeam.)

### 6. Plaza-center siting for utility structures (extends technique 8)
- **Principle:** Technique 11 stages an *arrival* for buildings with fronts. Civic utility structures invert it: wells, fountains, statues, and bell towers have no front — they sit at the CENTER of open space or path crossings with 360° access, and the design serves all sides equally. There is no "back of a well."
- **Recipe:** Place at a path intersection, plaza center, or open green; keep a 2–3 block clear ring around the plinth; paths may approach from any/all directions. Detail all faces identically — if you wouldn't put it on the north face, don't put it on the south face either.
- **Apply when:** wells, fountains, statues, bell towers, notice boards. **NOT when:** the structure belongs to a building (a wall fountain, a gate lantern) — those follow the building's facing, not the plaza's.
- **Prevents:** the tucked-away well — pushed against a wall or into a corner it reads as clutter the town is hiding, not infrastructure the town uses. (Well ref: centered on open grass, demo platform's other builds at distance, approached from all sides.)

### 7. The dark shaft below (NEW — partially inferred, flagged)
- **Principle:** The bucket's whole story collapses if what hangs below it is bright solid platform. A well needs visible *depth*: the eye must believe water (or darkness) is down there. This is the one part of the grammar the frame does not clearly show — the platform reads solid from this angle — so treat the construction as inferred from well grammar, not observed.
- **Recipe:** Under the bucket, open a 1×1 or 2×2 shaft: water source blocks if the well is "working"; if dry or stylized, recess dark blocks (deepslate, black concrete) at least 2 blocks down so the hole reads as depth, not paint. Rim the shaft mouth with a contrasting ring (stone-brick ring on a slab platform) so the opening reads as intentional.
- **Apply when:** every well, always — no exceptions. A well without a shaft is a bucket display stand. **NOT when:** — there is no not; if you can't afford the shaft, don't build the well.
- **Prevents:** the "bucket over nothing" failure — the single fastest way to break the well illusion. (Well ref: shaft NOT clearly visible in frame — inferred; recommend verifying against the reel footage before the roast stage treats this as observed.)
