## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: bell tower ref (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/14-belltower.jpg`)

**Build observed (frame + transcript):** tall narrow stone tower; a real bell hangs inside from a heavy timber frame; open belfry top story (inferred — transcript); four pathways with four doors, one per face (inferred — transcript); interior is plain/unfinished ("looks kind of weird inside" — transcript); the build's purpose is to be seen from afar, never entered.

### 1. The bell yoke — a visible load-bearing frame for hanging objects (NEW)
- **Principle:** A bell (or any heavy hanging object) must read as HELD, not floating. A timber post-and-beam frame visibly chunkier than any decorative woodwork sells the weight — the viewer's brain accepts "that beam carries something heavy" instantly.
- **Recipe:** Inside the belfry (or chamber), build 2–4 vertical **dark oak log** posts, full chamber height, one at each corner of the hanging zone. Span them with a horizontal **dark oak log** beam across the top. Hang the bell from the beam's center: bell body (iron blocks / cauldron / custom) suspended by a **fence post or chain** 1–2 blocks below the beam. Posts must be full 1×1 logs minimum; if the span exceeds 3 blocks, double the posts (2×1) or add a second beam. The yoke wood should be the darkest timber in the build — contrast = "this is structural."
- **Apply when:** bells, hanging cages, chandeliers, crane hooks — anything heavy suspended in air. **NOT when:** lightweight hangings (lanterns, pendant rows — technique 6 already covers those; a yoke frame for a lantern reads as overbuilt nonsense).
- **Prevents:** floating bells/objects that read as creative-mode placement errors; bells hung from a single fence that read as "about to fall." (Bell tower ref — the dark-oak beam frame is clearly visible in-frame; the bell itself is inferred from the transcript.)

### 2. Open belfry crown — the top story with no walls (extends technique 4)
- **Principle:** Technique 4's belvedere grows a shaft *out of* a roof; the belfry is the inverse crown for towers — REMOVE the top story's walls instead of adding mass. Four corner posts + open arches + the bell visible between them gives a silhouette with three readable layers (posts, bell, cap) at zero extra footprint, and the void between posts is free silhouette complexity.
- **Recipe:** Top story (3–4 tall): 4 corner pillars of the shaft material (**stone bricks**), full story height. Between pillars, leave every face OPEN — no wall fill. Run a 1-block **slab or stair** arch/lintel course across the top of each open face (flat lintel is fine; a 2-step stair arch reads more "belfry"). Hang the bell from the yoke beam (technique 1) centered in the void. Cap with a small pyramid or pitched **stair-block** roof overhanging the pillars by 1 block. Optional: one **lantern** hung beside the bell — lit belfry at night is a landmark beacon.
- **Apply when:** bell towers, watchtowers, lighthouse tops, signal towers — any tower whose job is landmark/signaling. **NOT when:** the top story must be enclosed (living space, storage, dovecote) — then use technique 4's belvedere or a solid cap instead.
- **Prevents:** solid-capped tower tops reading as "flat top with a hat" (the exact anti-pattern); towers where the bell is hidden inside a closed box, wasting the single most interesting feature. (Bell tower ref — open belfry + real bell inferred from transcript; the in-frame timber frame is consistent with a yoke inside an open chamber.)

### 3. Landmark siting — design for the 50-block view (extends technique 8)
- **Principle:** Technique 8 sits builds *in* terrain; landmarks need a different siting logic — nobody walks up to them, so the 5-block detail budget (window recipes, shutter states, arrival staging) is wasted. Spend it on: (a) a high-contrast top feature readable at range, (b) strong vertical banding, (c) 4-way symmetry since it's seen from all sides.
- **Recipe:** Shaft: 4-way symmetric (square or octagonal), one material family, with a single vertical accent readable at distance — darker **pilaster strips** (technique 3) at corners, or a **trim band** every ~8 blocks so the shaft has rhythm at range. Top: the belfry/bell (technique 2) or equivalent single bold feature — never two competing tops. Ground: keep the immediate base CLEAR (no prop clusters, no hedges crowding it) for a 3–5 block radius so the silhouette reads clean against terrain; paths may cross the clearing but nothing vertical should stand in it.
- **Apply when:** bell towers, lighthouses, monuments, waypoints, border markers. **NOT when:** the build is meant to be entered and used — then the full technique stack (7, 11) applies and this discipline would starve it.
- **Prevents:** a landmark that reads as a plain pillar from afar because the entire detail budget was spent on door-height features nobody sees; competing tops that blur into one gray mass at distance. (Bell tower ref — "designed to be seen from afar, not entered," four symmetric approaches — transcript; in-frame doorway shows the plain shaft base.)

### 4. Interior honesty — don't decorate what won't be seen (NEW)
- **Principle:** The transcript's verdict — "it looks kind of weird inside" — is not a flaw, it's correct budgeting. Every block spent on an unseen interior is stolen from the exterior, which is the only thing anyone ever sees. An AI builder should treat "will a player ever stand here?" as a budget gate.
- **Recipe:** For exterior-only builds: interior = structural minimum only — the support frame (technique 1), the access stair (technique 5), plain shaft walls inside (no noise banding needed — technique 1's 30% rule is for SEEN surfaces), no floor finishes, no lighting design, no rooms or furniture. Put 100% of the saved budget into the exterior silhouette and siting. Document the decision: "interior unfinished by design — landmark, not building."
- **Apply when:** landmarks, monuments, facades, diorama backdrops, anything the player cannot or will not enter. **NOT when:** the player enters the build (houses, inns, shops, towers with visitable tops) — technique 7's interior rules apply in full.
- **Prevents:** the classic AI-builder failure of fully furnishing a bell tower's interior while its exterior stays a plain stone box — budget spent exactly where no eye ever goes. (Bell tower ref — in-frame: plain stone walls, bare support frame, simple stair, zero decoration; transcript confirms the interior is unfinished and that's fine.)

### 5. Wall-hugging stair runs for narrow shafts (extends technique 13)
- **Principle:** Technique 13 governs ornament on tall-narrow builds; circulation is the functional gap it leaves. In a ≤5×5 shaft, a spiral is fiddly and an exterior stair reads as scaffolding — the buildable answer is straight flights hugging the interior walls with a 1×1 landing at each corner turn.
- **Recipe:** Per story: one straight **stair-block** flight (same material as the shaft walls — here **stone brick stairs**) running along one interior wall, 1 wide, rising the full story height; a 1×1 **slab** landing in the corner; next flight along the adjacent wall. Keep the stair tight to the wall (no gap — a gap reads as a separate structure). No railing needed inside a 1-wide shaft (walls are the railing).
- **Apply when:** any tower/shaft ≤5×5 needing believable vertical access (belfries, watchtowers, lighthouse stairs). **NOT when:** the shaft is ≥7×7 (room for a proper stairwell — build one); exterior feature stairs (those are technique 7's ladder/porch vocabulary, different job).
- **Prevents:** towers with no believable way up — breaks the "someone maintains this" read; exterior staircases stapled onto a landmark shaft. (Bell tower ref — in-frame: stone stairs ascending tight along the interior shaft wall.)

### 6. Doorway voids on unentered builds (extends technique 7)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's window/door recipes assume an approached, entered facade. On a landmark shaft, a doorway is a dark void — and the void IS the detail. An unlit interior behind a plain opening gives all the contrast a shutter assembly would, for zero blocks.
- **Recipe:** Openings 2 wide × 3 tall, **no doors installed** (or open fence-gate leaves if a barrier read is needed). Recess the opening 1 block into the shaft. Leave the interior behind it dark — no lantern within 3 blocks of the opening. No trim, no shutters, no lantern above: the black rectangle against stone is the finished look.
- **Apply when:** landmark shafts, monuments, crypts, any build the player doesn't enter. **NOT when:** the interior behind is lit (a lit void needs the shutter-state recipe — an untrimmed glowing hole reads as a mistake); on entered buildings (use technique 7's full door recipe).
- **Prevents:** fussy door detailing (frames, lanterns, shutters) on a shaft nobody approaches — detail that only adds noise at the one scale that matters (distance). (Bell tower ref — in-frame: plain open doorway, dark interior beyond, zero trim; consistent with an unentered shaft.)

### 7. Four-way path convergence for freestanding landmarks (extends technique 11)
- **Principle:** Technique 11's arrival sequence (threshold → cluster → hero prop) is for buildings you ENTER — applying it to a landmark stages an arrival that never happens. The landmark variant is simpler: paths converge from all four directions, plain, so the tower reads as important from every side instead of having a "front" and three "backs."
- **Recipe:** Run a **dirt path** (2 wide, no edging needed) to each face of the landmark, on-axis with each door/face, extending until it meets the terrain's natural path network. NO gate thresholds, NO prop clusters, NO hero prop — technique 11's stages (a)–(c) are explicitly skipped; only the "keep the path clear" discipline (d) carries over. Where paths meet the clearing (technique 3's 3–5 block radius), let them feather out — no hard border.
- **Apply when:** freestanding landmarks, monuments, waypoints. **NOT when:** the build has one real entrance (converging four paths on it confuses the arrival read — use technique 11 properly instead); on buildings with a designed front/back.
- **Prevents:** a landmark with a single path that reads as "someone's backyard shed" from three directions; arrival-staging props (cart, crates) stranded beside a tower nobody enters. (Bell tower ref — four pathways + four doors inferred from transcript; in-frame dirt path visible outside the doorway.)

### 8. Weatherproof shell, sheltered structure — material semantics by exposure (NEW)
- **Principle:** The guide's technique 1 covers material *variation*; this is material *semantics*: on an exposed monumental build, the exterior is 100% masonry (nothing to rot, fade, or read as unfinished scaffolding at distance) while timber is reserved for the sheltered interior structure, where its warmth is visible only up close. Exposure decides the material, not taste.
- **Recipe:** Landmark shaft exterior: single masonry family (**stone bricks** + technique 1's 30% mossy/cracked noise) — zero wood on the outside face, including trim, sills, and crowns (use stone stairs/slabs for all of them). Interior/sheltered: timber allowed and encouraged for the support frame (technique 1's yoke), stair treads, and belfry beams — that's where wood belongs because it's protected and seen only at close range.
- **Apply when:** exposed landmarks, monuments, fortifications, any build whose exterior must read clean at 50+ blocks. **NOT when:** the timber frame IS the aesthetic (Tudor houses, technique 2's skeleton-first builds — there the wood is the point, and the build is viewed up close).
- **Prevents:** wood trim on a tall exposed tower reading as unfinished scaffolding or a construction site at distance; stone-only interiors that feel dead where the player actually stands close. (Bell tower ref — in-frame: dark-oak structural frame lives entirely inside the stone shaft; exterior inferred stone-only from transcript's "stone tower" description.)
