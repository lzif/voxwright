## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: Fountain ref (`~/workspace/disruptive-reel/frames/08-fountain.jpg`)

**Build observed:** town-center water plaza — a ~13×13 raised stone-brick basin with water set below rim level, crossed by plus-shaped oak-plank walkways at rim height, a 4-post wooden canopy at the center of the cross, 4 identical lantern-on-stone-pillar fixtures at the walkway's inner corners, stair-chamfered basin rim, and stone-slab paths radiating outward on all four walkway axes.

### 1. Basin-first water architecture (NEW)
- **Principle:** A fountain is architecture that contains water; a pond is a hole that holds it. Building the container first — a raised rim with the water set 1–2 blocks below the rim top — makes the water read as designed, not dug. The rim is the technique; the water is just the fill.
- **Recipe:** Outer rim of **stone bricks**, ~13×13, **2 blocks high** above the basin floor; water fills the bottom 1–2 (rim top stands ~1 block above the surrounding path). Chamfer the square: at each rim corner, replace the 90° turn with 2–3 **stone-brick stair** blocks set at 45°, giving a rounded-octagon plan. Basin floor: stone slabs or stone bricks (visible through shallow water). Rim material must contrast the water — pale stone against blue water, never blue-on-blue.
- **Apply when:** town plazas, courtyards, formal grounds — anywhere water must read as architecture. **NOT when:** rustic/wilderness settings (a built basin in a forest reads as a swimming pool — build a pond instead); tiny plots where a 13-wide basin dwarfs everything (see technique 5 below).
- **Prevents:** the "dug hole with water" look — square water pits that read as unfinished excavation. (Fountain ref: raised stone rim, chamfered corners, water visibly below rim top.)

### 2. Walkable cross over water (NEW)
- **Principle:** A water feature in a circulation hub must be traversable, not skirted — otherwise the plaza's center becomes an obstacle and foot traffic routes around the very thing meant to anchor it. Plus-shaped walkways at rim level turn the basin into a bridge-plaza: visitors cross *through* the water feature.
- **Recipe:** **2-wide oak-plank walkways** (1-wide reads as a plank, not a promenade) along both midlines of the basin, deck surface flush with the rim top / surrounding paths — no step up, no step down. Span is short (basin walls carry it); no support posts needed under ~7-block spans. Walkways must exit the basin on all four sides, continuing as paths.
- **Apply when:** any water feature placed at a path convergence (plaza hubs, courtyard centers). **NOT when:** the water is meant to be viewed, not crossed (reflecting pools, walled-garden basins — there, a perimeter walk suffices); basins <7 wide (the cross eats the water — use a single bridge or none).
- **Prevents:** "pretty obstacle" syndrome — a centerpiece fountain that blocks the circulation it was built to organize. (Fountain ref: plank cross at rim height, water visible on all four quadrants beneath it.)

### 3. Center canopy as the plaza's vertical anchor (extends technique 4's "one vertical accent" + technique 10's anchor logic)
- **Principle:** A flat water plaza has the same failure mode as a flat roof: an empty lid. Technique 4 demands one vertical accent to break an outline; on a water plaza the accent goes at the cross center — a small 4-post canopy that gives the eye a destination and the plaza a silhouette from every approach.
- **Recipe:** 3×3 footprint at the walkway crossing: 4 **dark-oak log posts** (inferred species — reads as dark timber; any contrasting log works), 3 tall; hip roof of **oak stairs** in 2 tiers, overhanging the posts by 1 block (technique 4's overhang rule). Total height ~5–6 above the deck. Open on all four sides — it's a shelter, not a room.
- **Apply when:** any flat plaza/water feature ≥9 wide with no other vertical element. **NOT when:** the basin already carries a central vertical fountain jet/column (one accent per plaza — canopy XOR column, never both); basins <9 wide (the canopy crowds out the water — the water IS the feature at small scale).
- **Prevents:** the water plaza reading as an empty stone tray — the "hat with no head" problem applied to water. (Fountain ref: 4-post timber canopy with stepped hip roof at the exact center of the walkway cross.)

### 4. Corner lantern pillars on the structure's rhythm (extends technique 6: lighting rhythm)
- **Principle:** Technique 6's rule — identical fixtures on the structure's rhythm, never scattered torches — applied to a water plaza: the fixtures sit at the walkway cross's inner corners, so light and circulation share one geometry. Four identical pillars beat eight random torches.
- **Recipe:** At the 4 inner corners where the walkways meet the central 3×3, place a **2–3-high stone-brick pillar** (reads as stacked stone-brick walls or pillars — exact block inferred; any pale masonry works) with a **lantern** on top. All four identical, all four at the same height. Nothing else lights the basin — no torches on the rim, no glowstone in the water.
- **Apply when:** any structured water plaza or courtyard basin. **NOT when:** the plaza is day-use only by design (then skip lighting entirely rather than half-lighting it); alongside eave/pendant lighting on the same rhythm — one system per space (technique 6's guard).
- **Prevents:** a dead black hole at the town center after dark; the mob-proofing look of torches slapped on the rim. (Fountain ref: 4 matching lantern pillars, one per quadrant corner.)

### 5. Four-way path radiation — the plaza hub (extends technique 8: siting; town-scale cousin of technique 11)
- **Principle:** Technique 11 stages an arrival at a door; a town-center fountain stages arrivals for the whole town — the basin is the hub and paths are the spokes. Paths must leave on all four walkway axes, continuing the cross's lines outward, so the fountain *organizes* circulation instead of merely decorating it.
- **Recipe:** **2–3-wide stone-slab paths** extending from each basin side's midpoint, aligned with the walkway axes — the walkway lines continue uninterrupted from basin to town. Paths start at the rim edge (no gap, no grass strip between basin and path). Each path should lead *somewhere* visible (a build, a gate, the next hub) — a path to nowhere is technique 11's "gate mid-path" failure at town scale.
- **Apply when:** any town-center or grounds-center water feature. **NOT when:** the fountain is a decorative garden object (walled garden, manor courtyard — 1–2 paths suffice; four spokes in a private garden reads as a roundabout); when surrounding builds don't exist yet — lay the paths when the town does, not before.
- **Prevents:** disconnected-landmark syndrome — a beautiful centerpiece nobody walks to because no path claims it. (Fountain ref: slab paths leaving the basin on all four sides along the walkway axes.)

### 6. Fountain-vs-pond decision rule (NEW — meta-technique for the builder)
- **Principle:** The pond agent and this frame show the same town-planning move (water at a path convergence) executed two ways. The choice is not aesthetic preference — it's context: fountains are architecture, ponds are nature, and putting the wrong one in reads as a category error no amount of detailing fixes.
- **Recipe (the decision):** Build a **fountain** when the setting is formal/symmetric (town plaza, courtyard, manor grounds), paths converge geometrically, and surrounding builds are masonry/timber — use basin-first construction (technique 1), geometric plan, built rim. Build a **pond** when the setting is organic (village edge, park, wilderness), paths wander, and surroundings are natural — dug irregular shape, grass/dirt edging, lily pads and reeds, no built rim. Never mix the languages: a chamfered stone rim on an irregular muddy pond, or reeds in a geometric basin, reads as indecision.
- **Apply when:** any water feature is planned at a path convergence — decide FIRST, then build. **NOT when:** the water is purely functional (fishing dock, farm irrigation — function dictates form there).
- **Prevents:** the most expensive failure in this category — a fully built, fully detailed water feature in the wrong formal language, which no trim or lighting can save. (Fountain ref: geometric basin, built rim, slab paths — the formal answer to the pond's organic one.)
