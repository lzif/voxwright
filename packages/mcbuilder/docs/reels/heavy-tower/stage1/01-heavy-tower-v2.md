## 1. Machicolation gallery band — the projecting defensive course (NEW — HIGH)

**Principle:** T7's parapet is just an alternating high/low rim, and T5's base
crenellation is the same verb at the bottom. Neither gives the *projecting
gallery*: a full course that juts past the wall face, topped by a parapet,
with a row of openings hanging below it. The projection + the opening row +
the shadow gap underneath are what sell "fortified" — a flat parapet on a
flush wall reads as trim; the gallery reads as a fighting platform.

**Recipe:** At each stage transition, run a 1-block-proud course around the
full perimeter, top it with a crenellated parapet (alternating merlons), and
cut a row of openings directly below the projecting course — dark/inset so
they read as murder holes, not windows. The band must project: flush
openings under a flush parapet are just a window row (T7), not a gallery.

**CORRECTION vs v1 — the two bands are NOT identical:** v1 described the
band repeated at every transition with pointed arches. The dense set shows
the opening shape varies by level:
- **Lower band (stage 1 → 2): pointed arches**, 2–4 tall, spaced 2–3 apart
  (f0004, f0005, f0006, f0014, f0016, f0019).
- **Upper band (stage 2 → 3): small square murder-holes**, a row of small
  square openings under the parapet (f0007, f0008, f0016, f0017).
  Do NOT copy-paste the pointed-arch row at every transition — varying the
  opening shape by level is part of the grammar.

**Apply when:** fortress/keep/castle towers, defensive walls, any build whose
theme is "can withstand attack" (the reel's own framing). **NOT when:**
domestic builds (a machicolation band on a cottage reads as cosplay); more
than one band language per build (gallery XOR T7's plain parapet on the same
tower — the gallery already contains a parapet); on shafts <5 wide (the
projection eats the wall).

**Prevents:** the "flat parapet" read — a crenellated rim with nothing under
it looks pasted on; the gallery gives the parapet a reason to exist.
Cross-ref T15 for arch construction instead of re-specifying it.

## 2. Stepped tower staging — narrow in steps, cap each step (NEW — HIGH)

**Principle:** T4's compound hierarchy composes *multiple* towers (flankers +
one dominant); T13's stacking discipline governs ornament on *narrow* (≤6×6)
towers. Neither covers a single WIDE tower that narrows in discrete stages:
wide base shaft → narrower second stage → narrower third stage → spire,
each stage capped with its own gallery/parapet. The staging is the
silhouette: without the steps the tower is an extruded box; with them it
reads as engineered mass.

**Recipe:** Build the tower in 3 stages (2 minimum): each stage 1–2 blocks
narrower per side than the one below, each stage transition marked by a
full-perimeter gallery band (candidate 1) or at minimum a proud cap course +
parapet. Stage heights: base stage tallest (~40–50% of shaft height), upper
stages progressively shorter. The top stage is the narrowest and carries the
crown grammar (spire) — never another full stage. Keep the total footprint
wide (≥8×8 base) — on ≤6×6 footprints this collapses into T13's territory
and the steps read as stacking, not staging.

**Massing guard (folded in from v1 candidate 5, per v1 roast FIX):** on a
staged wide tower, the shaft gets noise + slit grid only — no balconies,
awnings, bays, or trim bands — and the entire ornament budget goes to the
transitions (galleries) and the crown. If a face starts accumulating detail
beyond noise + slits, the massing has broken — strip it back.

**Apply when:** fortress towers, keeps, lighthouse-style monuments, any
single tower meant to read "heavy". **NOT when:** narrow wizard/fantasy
towers (use T13 + a loggia/belfry crown); when the build needs a flat roof
(staging forces a stepped silhouette — pick one); more than 4 stages (reads
as a wedding cake).

**Prevents:** the extruded-box tower — one unbroken shaft from ground to
spire, however tall, reads as a stretched cube; the stages are what give it
designed mass. (Dense ref: f0009, f0014, f0016, f0017, f0018, f0019 — three
clear stages + spire from every angle.)

## 3. Stepped dark spire with accent + finial — the heavy crown (NEW — HIGH)

**Principle:** T4 covers spires only as a generic "one vertical accent" and
pyramid roofs at *pavilion* scale (stepped stair roof, slab + finial cap).
The heavy tower's spire is a different animal: a tall (roughly as tall as the
stages below it) dark-stone stepped taper that contrasts the pale shaft,
carrying exactly one accent block near the tip and a thin finial spike — plus
one subordinate spire at ~60% height on a side turret. The dark-vs-pale
contrast + the single accent are what make it a crown rather than a roof.

**Recipe:** Spire material: a dark stone family contrasting the shaft (dark
deepslate/stone against pale stone brick). Profile: stepped taper — inset 1
block per 2–3 blocks of rise (steeper than a pyramid roof's 1:1, so the
spire reads tall, not squat; ratio still estimated, not block-counted).
Near the tip (2–4 blocks below the finial), place exactly ONE accent block
in a saturated color (red in the reel) — one block, never a band. Finish
with a thin finial: 1×1 dark spike 2–4 tall. Subordinate spire (optional, at
most one): same construction at ~60% height on a corner/side turret, no
accent block — the accent belongs to the main spire only. The subordinate
turret may carry a small arched window (f0011).

**Apply when:** fortress/keep towers, dark-fantasy crowns, any tower whose
shaft is pale and needs a dark crown to terminate. **NOT when:** the shaft
is already dark (no contrast — the spire vanishes into the shaft);
pale/light spires (that's T4's pyramid roof, not this); more than one accent
block (two red blocks read as a mistake, not a detail).

**Prevents:** the flat-stop failure (T13's named enemy) on wide towers — and
the "spire as afterthought" (a 3-block pyramid cap on a 30-block tower reads
as a hat, not a crown). (Dense ref: f0009, f0010, f0011, f0016, f0017, f0018,
f0019 — dark taper + exactly one red block + thin finial + subordinate
spire, confirmed from every angle.)

## 4. Spire-base arcade — pale-framed openings IN the spire (NEW — MED-HIGH)

**NEW in v2 — the dense set caught what the 8-frame set missed.** The dark
spire's lower portion carries small arched openings with pale stone frames,
punched directly into the taper (f0012, f0016, f0017, f0018, f0019). The pale
frames contrast the dark spire stone, so the openings read as a deliberate
arcade, not damage.

**Why it is not just part of candidate 3:** candidate 3's spire is a solid
taper + accent + finial. The arcade breaks the "spire = solid mass"
assumption: even the crown's taper carries openings, and it does so in the
shaft's pale material, tying the dark crown back to the pale tower. It is
also distinct from candidate 1's gallery arches (those hang below a
projecting course; these are flush in the taper) and from T7's windows (no
glass, no shutters, arched, pale surround).

**Recipe:** In the spire's lower third, punch 2–4 small arched openings (1–2
wide, 2–3 tall) with pale-stone surrounds, spaced around the taper. Keep
them small and few — the spire must still read as mass first, arcade second.

**Apply when:** tall dark spires on pale towers (with candidate 3).
**NOT when:** short spires (no room — the arcade crowds the taper); pale
spires (no frame contrast — the openings vanish).

**Confidence note:** MED-HIGH — visible in 5 dense frames, but small at
distance; exact opening count per face not resolvable. The roast stage may
fold this into candidate 3 as a spire detail instead of a standalone
technique; either way the observation is frame-true.

## 5. Paired slit window grid — fortification windows (NEW — HIGH)

**Principle:** T7's window recipe is domestic (glass panes + shutters +
flower box, repeated in vertical columns). T13's quiet-shaft corollary allows
at most one narrow slit per face on landmark shafts. The heavy tower does
neither: its windows are *pairs* of 1-wide × 2-tall dark slits (no glass, no
shutters, no trim), repeated in a strict grid — several pairs per face,
aligned in rows and columns across the whole shaft. The pairing + the grid
are the grammar: single slits read as arrow loops; paired slits in a grid
read as a fortress's window system.

**Recipe:** Windows as pairs: two 1-wide × 2-tall dark openings (air or dark
blocks, never glass) spaced 1 block apart, pairs spaced 3–4 blocks apart
horizontally and 4–6 vertically, aligned to a strict grid on every face. No
sills, no shutters, no lintels — the grid alignment IS the detail. On the
upper stages the pairs sit between pilaster ribs (candidate 7's bays); on
the main shaft they sit on the flat wall.

**Dense-set confirmation:** the grid covers stages 1 AND 2 (f0007, f0013,
f0014, f0016, f0017, f0019); stage 1 shows 3 rows (f0014, f0019). This is
standalone, NOT a T7 extension (per v1 roast): it inverts every detail of
T7's domestic recipe and carries an explicit scope override of T13's
quiet-shaft corollary, valid for wide fortress towers only.

**Note (LOW):** one lone single slit appears between pairs in f0013 — the
grid is pairs-dominant, not pairs-exclusive. Do not over-police this; a lone
slit (stairwell, door) does not break the grid.

**Apply when:** fortress/keep/castle shafts, defensive walls, any wide tower
where windows must read as fortification, not domesticity. **NOT when:**
domestic builds (use T7's full window recipe); narrow landmark shafts (T13's
quiet-shaft corollary — one slit per face — still governs there); when the
wall already carries heavy ornament (grid + ornament = noise).

**Prevents:** the "house windows on a castle" failure — glass + shutters on
a fortress shaft kill the fortified read instantly.

## 7. Pilaster ribs — same-material vertical strips (DUPLICATE — confirms T3)

**Observation:** the upper stages carry 1-wide vertical strips running
floor-to-roof with window bays between them (f0006, f0007, f0016, f0017).
T3's pilaster strips already specify exactly this: 1-wide vertical strips,
spaced 3–4 apart, strips on corners, bays between them. The only nuance is
that the reel's ribs are the *same* stone family as the wall (working as
shadow-line ribs) rather than T3's contrasting material — a same-material
variant worth a one-line note in T3, not a new technique.

**Verdict: DUPLICATE.** Do not propose as new.
