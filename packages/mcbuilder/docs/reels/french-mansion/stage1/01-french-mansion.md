## Stage 1 — Pattern identification: Life Laps French Mansion (`~/workspace/french-mansion-reel/frames/`)

**Build observed:** a large 3-story formal mansion, pale cream walls on a stone base course under a dark mansard roof, set in terraced hillside grounds above water. The "French" identity is carried by the roof (steep dark stair-block mansard slope + dormers + flat top deck) and by a central projecting pavilion with a triangular pediment over a grand entrance stair. Walls are deliberately quiet: tall windows in strict columns with pilasters between bays, one window recipe everywhere. Grounds are architecture: parterre beds, cone topiary, a circular fountain on axis, a hedge maze with white statues, a low white balustrade fence with gate piers. Interiors are furnished (cutaway timelapse shows dining rooms, bedrooms); the video ends in an indoor orangery of stepped moss planters, topiary, and roses.

### 1. Mansard roof with dormers (NEW — recipe-level; T4 names dormers but gives no construction)
- **Principle:** Technique 4's roof entries cover steep stair-block roofs, cross-gables, and belvederes, and "vary the roofline" *mentions* dormers — but no entry gives the mansard construction: a dual-pitch roof where the steep slope IS the top story's wall. The mansard is what makes a big pale box read as "French" instead of "generic mansion" — the roof is a full story tall, so it carries silhouette, windows, and identity at once.
- **Recipe:** Lower slope: dark stair blocks (deepslate-tile family) at ~60–70° for 4–6 blocks of rise on all four sides, overhanging the wall plate by 1 block. Upper deck: flat or near-flat top (slabs or full blocks) set back from the slope's top edge — the flat area is the roof's "attic" and must read as a deliberate cap, not an unfinished hole (finish its rim with a fascia course or a low parapet). Dormers: at 2–4 regular intervals along each long slope, punch a small gabled projection through the slope — 2-wide × 2–3-tall pale-wall cheeks + a 1–2-wide window + a mini stair-block gable cap that ties back into the slope. Dormer cheeks in the WALL material (pale), not the roof material — the contrast is what makes them pop against the dark slope. Keep dormer width ≤ ~1/3 of the bay spacing or the slope reads as Swiss cheese.
- **Apply when:** formal mansions, chateaux, grand houses, any large pale-walled build that needs a roof with identity. **NOT when:** small builds (a mansard on a ≤9-wide house eats the whole facade — use T4's plain steep roof); timber/Tudor archetypes (the mansard is a masonry-formal grammar, it fights half-timbering); when the roof already carries a belvedere (one roof-crown grammar per roof — mansard dormers XOR belvedere, never both competing).
- **Prevents:** the "big pale box" read — a large formal house with a plain gable roof looks like a warehouse; the mansard slope + dormers give it a face. (Life Laps ref: frame 04 — steep dark slope + flat top deck + cross-gabled wings; frame 05 — dormers breaking the front slope, pale cheeks against dark stairs; frame 07 — dormers on the side slope, lit windows at dusk.)

### 2. Central pedimented pavilion + grand stair (NEW)
- **Principle:** Technique 11 stages the *approach* (gate, props, hero prop) but says nothing about the facade's own centerpiece. A formal mansion needs its entrance to be a mass, not a door: the middle bay projects past the facade, rises through the eaves, and caps in a triangular pediment — the projection is what the eye lands on from 50 blocks, and the wide stair is what makes the door feel ceremonial rather than functional.
- **Recipe:** Project the central bay 1–2 blocks past the main facade, 3–5 bays wide (odd count so the door sits on-axis). Carry the pavilion's walls 1 story above the main eaves line (or through the mansard slope) and cap with a triangular pediment: stepped stair-block gable in the wall material, 3–5 blocks tall, with a small round or slit window centered in the gable. Entrance: arched or tall rectangular door, recessed 1 block, reached by a wide ceremonial stair — full pavilion width, 4–7 steps, flanked by low stone cheeks or balustrade stubs, landing on a small terrace at door level. Keep the pavilion's window recipe identical to the main facade's (same tall windows, same pilasters) — the projection + pediment do the differentiating, not new ornament.
- **Apply when:** formal mansions, civic buildings, any facade ≥15 wide with a single main entrance. **NOT when:** small or asymmetric builds (a pediment on a 9-wide cottage reads as a temple graft); two main entrances (a pavilion crowns ONE axis — twin entrances get twin bookend towers per T4 instead); when the facade already has a dominant tower (pavilion + tower fight for the center).
- **Prevents:** the "door in a wall" failure — a grand house whose entrance is just a darker rectangle among identical windows. (Life Laps ref: frame 05 — projecting central bay, triangular pediment with a small centered window, wide stair to an arched door, flanking terrace; frame 07 — the same pavilion from the side, rising through the eaves.)

### 3. Hedge maze garden (NEW)
- **Principle:** Technique 8's enclosed plots are *planted* rooms (beds, crops, flowers). A maze is a *walked* room: the hedges are walls you move through, not beds you look at. It gives formal grounds a second garden verb — the parterre is for viewing, the maze is for entering — and it fills a large flat garden area with almost no block variety (leaves + dirt paths).
- **Recipe:** Lay a rectangular maze ~15–25 blocks per side in 1-block-wide leaf hedges (oak/dark-oak leaves, 2 blocks tall — tall enough to block the view, low enough to read as garden, not fortress), with 1-block dirt/gravel paths between hedge lines. Design one entrance and one exit on different sides (a loop with no exit reads as a trap, not a garden); keep 2–3 small clearings inside (3×3, one with a white statue or fountain as a waypoint reward). Edge the whole maze with a low stone or pale curb so it reads as a designed object, not escaped hedges. Plant a few topiary cones at the outer corners to tie it to the parterre's language.
- **Apply when:** large formal grounds (estate, palace, manor gardens) with ≥15×15 of flat garden to spare. **NOT when:** small gardens (a 9×9 maze is a puzzle, not a garden — it reads as a hedge blob); wild/natural grounds (a maze is formal by definition — it fights T8's wild-water and ragged-edge language); when the grounds already have a second focal garden (maze XOR a second fountain court — one "destination garden" per estate, the parterre is the first).
- **Prevents:** the empty-lawn problem — big formal grounds with nothing between the parterre and the boundary read as a mowed field; the maze is the cheapest large-area garden fill that still rewards a visit. (Life Laps ref: frame 07 — full leaf-hedge maze with dirt paths, white statues as waypoints, pale curb edge, topiary at the corners.)

### 4. White balustrade boundary + gate piers (NEW; MED-CONFIDENCE — construction approximate)
- **Principle:** Technique 11's gate threshold is a single gate on the path axis; technique 7's parapet rules cover terrace edges. Neither gives the *grounds boundary*: a low, continuous, pale balustrade that says "this estate ends here" without hiding the garden behind it. The balustrade's job is enclosure with transparency — a solid wall would hide the parterre, a fence would read as farm, a hedge would read as wild.
- **Recipe (approximate — frames show it at distance):** low wall course (pale blocks: quartz/white concrete family) ~1 block tall with a baluster rhythm — alternating full blocks and fence/slab posts, or a top rail over regularly spaced piers — running along the garden boundary. At path crossings, break it with square gate piers: 2×2 pale pillars, 2–3 tall, capped with a slab or small pyramid, with the path passing between them (no gate leaf required — the piers ARE the threshold). Keep the balustrade low (≤1 block + rail) so the mansion stays visible over it from the approach.
- **Apply when:** formal estate boundaries, garden edges facing a public approach, any grounds that need a "designed edge" rather than a wall or hedge. **NOT when:** defensive builds (a 1-block balustrade on a castle reads as a mistake — use T4/T5 fortification language); small plots (a balustrade around a cottage garden reads as a dollhouse fence — use T5 hedges).
- **Prevents:** the "garden bleeds into wilderness" read — formal grounds with no edge look unfinished at the boundary, however good the beds are. (Life Laps ref: frames 05–06 — low white balustrade with square piers along the lawn edge and at path crossings; exact baluster block construction not resolvable at this distance — flagged MED.)
- **Inference flag:** baluster construction (fence posts vs. slabs vs. full blocks) is not frame-verifiable; the roast stage should treat the recipe's baluster detail as provisional and keep only the boundary + piers grammar if the detail can't be confirmed.

### 5. Indoor orangery — stepped moss planters + topiary (NEW; MED-CONFIDENCE — single interior frame)
- **Principle:** Technique 7's interiors cover room anchors, storage walls, and flower pots as the "alive" signal — but no entry gives a *garden room*: a room whose entire furnishing is planting. The orangery works because it inverts the normal interior rule (furniture first, plants as accents): here the planters ARE the furniture, stepped in height so the room reads as a terraced greenhouse rather than a storage room with pots.
- **Recipe:** Floor in dark planks. Along both side walls run stepped moss-block planters: 2–3 tiers stepping up toward the wall (each tier 1 block higher and 1 block deeper than the last), planted with leaf topiary (1–2-block leaf blobs on short stems), roses (red flowers), and occasional white flowers as accents. Keep a 2–3-wide clear plank path down the room's center — the path is what makes it a room you walk through, not a greenhouse you look into. Warm light only (lanterns or hidden glow, never bright white) so the green reads lush, not clinical.
- **Apply when:** mansion/palace interiors with a room to spare, conservatory/orangery/garden-room programs, any interior that needs a "wow" room without furniture cost. **NOT when:** small houses (an orangery in a 2-room cottage eats the whole interior budget — use T7's flower pots instead); survival-functional rooms (the orangery is pure display — never put the enchanting/smelting/storage core in it).
- **Prevents:** the "every room is a bedroom" interior — a large furnished house where every room has the same bed-table-shelf kit reads as a hotel; one garden room breaks the pattern for the cost of leaves and moss. (Life Laps ref: frame 08 — stepped moss planters both sides, leaf topiary, red roses + one white flower, dark plank center path, warm light.)
- **Inference flag:** single interior frame only; planter tier count (2–3) and flower placement are read from one angle — treat exact tier geometry as approximate.
